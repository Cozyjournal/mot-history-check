Your MOT Certificate Explained: VT20, VT30 and VT32

Last reviewed: November 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

Every MOT ends with a piece of paper, but the paper most people get is not the one they expect. There are three documents, and which one you are handed tells you the result before you read a word: a VT20 means the car passed, a VT30 means it failed, and a VT32 is the advisory notice that comes with a pass. Most guides mention the codes in passing and never explain what is actually written on each one, so drivers fold the sheet into the glovebox and miss the parts that matter, the expiry date, the mileage record, and the advisories that predict next year's fail. This guide reads each document line by line, in plain English, and explains when the paper matters and when the online record is what counts.

The three documents at a glance

VT20, the pass certificate. Issued when the car passes. It confirms the vehicle met the minimum standards on the day of the test, and it records the expiry date. Minor defects and advisories can be listed on it, the car still passes.

VT30, the refusal of an MOT test certificate. Issued when the car fails. It lists every dangerous and major defect that caused the fail. This is the failure notice, and it is the document that tells you what has to be fixed before the car can be retested.

VT32, the advisory notice. Issued alongside a pass when the tester records advisory items. It lists the things the tester wants you to know about: parts wearing close to their limit, or minor defects to fix soon. The car has passed, but the VT32 is the list that predicts next year's fail.

The codes are DVSA form numbers, and they have been in use since the current certificate design arrived in May 2018. The pass certificate and the failure notice share the same general layout; the VT30 simply has more text in the defects section, because it has to list everything that failed.

VT20: the pass certificate

The VT20 is the document you want. Here is what is on it, from the top down:

Test station details. The name and address of the test centre, and the DVSA-issued testing station number. That number is the useful bit: if you ever need to check whether a test was really done, or raise a question with the DVSA, it is the station number that identifies the centre.

Tester number. Below the station number sits the number of the individual tester who carried out the test.

Registration and VIN. Your number plate and the 17-character Vehicle Identification Number. Check both: a wrong VIN on the certificate is a problem worth raising with the test centre, because the VIN is how the record is tied to your car.

Mileage. The reading recorded at the time of the test. This matters more than most people realise, the MOT system builds a mileage timeline across every test the car has ever had, so a reading that jumps backwards is the first sign of a clocked car. Our guide to wrong mileage on the MOT record explains what to do if a reading looks wrong.

Class and test date. The vehicle class, class 4 for most cars, and the calendar date the test was carried out. The test date is not the expiry date: the certificate runs for 12 months from the test, and the expiry date is printed separately.

Defects. Any minor defects and advisories the tester recorded. A pass with minor defects is still a pass, and the items are recorded so you have notice of them before the next test. Our guide to MOT advisories explained explains how to read these and which ones to fix first.

One thing the VT20 does not do: it does not guarantee the car is roadworthy. It says the car met the minimum standards on the day it was tested. A car can pass in the morning and develop a fault in the afternoon, and the law still expects you to keep it roadworthy at all times.

VT30: the failure notice

The VT30 is the document nobody wants, and it is worth reading carefully, because it decides what happens next. It lists every defect that caused the fail, graded under the system the DVSA introduced in May 2018:

Dangerous. The defect is a direct risk to road safety or the environment. A car with a dangerous defect cannot be driven away from the test centre. Driving a vehicle that has failed because of a dangerous problem can mean a fine of up to £2,500, a ban from driving and 3 penalty points, and it can invalidate your insurance.

Major. A significant safety or emissions concern, but short of dangerous. The car fails, and you cannot use it on a public road in a failing condition, unless you are driving it directly to a repair centre or a pre-booked retest, and only if it is still roadworthy.

Minor and advisory items can also be listed on a VT30, but they are not what failed the car. Only dangerous and major defects cause a fail.

Each defect line carries a Reason for Rejection (RFR) code, a DVSA reference number for the item that failed. The code is mostly useful to the tester and to the DVSA, but if you are comparing quotes for the repair, the RFR tells each garage exactly what has to be fixed. Our guide to common MOT failure reasons explains the faults that come up most often, and our guide to what to do if your car fails its MOT covers the retest rules, including the free partial retest within 10 working days if you leave the car at the centre.

VT32: the advisory notice

The VT32 is the quietest of the three documents, and the one most drivers ignore. It is issued with a pass when the tester records advisory items, things observed during the test that do not meet the threshold for a defect, but that the tester wants you to know about.

Typical advisory items are brake pads wearing thin, tyres close to the legal tread limit, slight play in a suspension joint, or a small oil leak. None of them fails the car today. All of them are the items most likely to fail it next year, and they are also the items a buyer looks at first when checking a used car's history. Fixing advisories before the next test is usually cheaper than fixing the fail they turn into, because a worn part caught early is a repair, while a worn part left alone can take other parts with it.

If your VT32 lists a minor defect, the same logic applies: a minor defect is a real fault that is not yet a safety risk. Get it fixed soon, and note it in the car's service history, a clean advisory record is one of the things that makes a car easier to sell.

The paper vs the online record

Here is the part that surprises most drivers: the paper certificate is a copy, not the record. When the test is done, the result is recorded in the official DVSA database straight away. That database entry is what counts, it is what the police check, what the tax system checks, and what a buyer sees. Losing the paper does not change your MOT status or its expiry date, and you do not need the paper to tax the car.

That is why the first thing to do with any certificate is to check it against the record. You can check a car's MOT history free on GOV.UK using just the registration number, and see every test, the result, the defects recorded and the mileage at each test. If the paper in your hand does not match the record, the record is right, and if you are buying a car, the record is the document to trust. Our guide to how to check MOT history shows the step-by-step process.

To see where each test was done, or to download a replacement paper certificate, GOV.UK asks for the 11-digit reference number from the V5C logbook. That safeguard stops strangers from looking up where your car was tested, while letting the registered keeper see everything.

Lost your certificate?

Replacing one is easy, and the online copy is free. With the registration number and the 11-digit V5C reference number, you can view, save or print a replacement from GOV.UK in a few minutes. Any MOT centre can also print a copy, for up to £10. Our guide to a lost MOT certificate walks through both routes, including what to do if you do not have the V5C reference number.

Can you appeal what the certificate says?

Yes, if you think the result was wrong, but do not have any repairs done until the appeal is finished. If the car failed, discuss the result with the test centre first, then send the complaint form to the DVSA within 14 working days of the test. If the car passed but you think it should have failed, the limits are 28 days for most defects and 3 months for corrosion. The DVSA will contact you within 5 days, and if it decides to recheck the car you will need to arrange a date and pay the full test fee again for a failed test, refunded if the appeal succeeds. For a passed test, there is no fee for the recheck.

Quick answers

What is a VT20 MOT certificate?

The VT20 is the MOT test pass certificate. It confirms the vehicle passed, and it records the test station, the tester, the registration and VIN, the mileage at the test, the vehicle class, the test date and the expiry date. Minor defects and advisories can be listed on it too, the car still passes.

What is a VT30 certificate?

The VT30 is the refusal of an MOT test certificate, the failure notice. It lists every dangerous and major defect that made the car fail. If a dangerous defect is listed, you must not drive the car away; driving with a dangerous defect can mean a fine of up to £2,500, 3 penalty points and a ban.

What is a VT32?

The VT32 is the advisory notice that comes with a pass when the tester records advisory items. It lists things the tester wants you to know about, wear that is not yet a fail, or minor defects to fix soon. The car has passed, but the items on the VT32 are the ones most likely to fail it next year.

Do I still need the paper MOT certificate?

Not for tax or for proving the car has an MOT, the official record sits in the DVSA database, and that is what counts. You do not need the paper to tax the car, and buyers can check the history free on GOV.UK. The paper is still useful as a quick reference and when selling, and a replacement is free to download from GOV.UK.

How do I check if an MOT certificate is genuine?

Check the car's MOT history free on GOV.UK using just the registration number. The official record shows every test, the result, the defects and the mileage, if the certificate in your hand does not match the record, it is not genuine.

To sum up: the VT20 is your pass certificate, the VT30 is the failure notice, and the VT32 is the advisory note that comes with a pass. Read the expiry date and the mileage on the VT20, read every defect line on the VT30 before you decide whether the car can be driven, and do not ignore the VT32, its advisories are next year's fails. And remember that the paper is only a copy: the real record lives in the DVSA database, it is free to check on GOV.UK, and a replacement certificate is free to download if the paper goes missing.
