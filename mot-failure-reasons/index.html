Common MOT failure reasons and how to avoid them

Last reviewed: November 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

Vehicles fail their MOT when an inspector identifies one or more faults classified as major or dangerous under official Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency standards. Many of the most frequent reasons for an inspection refusal are minor maintenance issues—such as blown lightbulbs or empty screenwash—that owners can readily identify and resolve before the test begins. For the full list of items the test covers, see our guide to what an MOT checks.

Understanding how test items are inspected and how defects are categorised enables motorists to save time and avoid re-inspection fees. Below, we break down the four official defect severity grades, examine the primary vehicle systems evaluated during the annual inspection, and provide an actionable DIY pre-test checklist.

Common MOT failures — the short version:

  • Most common reason: lights and electrics — about a quarter of all failures, usually just a blown bulb.
  • Most common single fault: a tyre worn below the 1.6mm legal tread limit.
  • How many fail: about one car in four fails its MOT at the first attempt.
  • The good news: most failures are cheap wear-and-tear items you can spot at home in 10 minutes — bulbs, wipers, screenwash and tyre tread.

The 5 most common MOT failure reasons

DVSA test data shows the same five areas cause most failures year after year. The figures below are the share of failed tests with at least one fault in each category, from DVSA MOT testing data for 2023 analysed by Green Flag. One failed test can have faults in several categories, so the shares do not add up to 100%.

Failure area Share of failed tests What usually fails Can you check it at home?
1. Lamps, reflectors & electrics 25% Blown headlamp, brake light or indicator bulbs; headlamp aim; number plate lamp Yes — switch everything on and walk around the car
2. Suspension 19% Broken coil springs, worn ball joints and bushes Partly — listen for knocks and check the car sits level
3. Brakes 16% Worn pads and discs, weak handbrake, ABS faults Partly — listen for squealing and check the pedal feel
4. Tyres 13% Tread below 1.6mm, cuts, bulges and exposed cords Yes — the 20p coin test and a look at the sidewalls
5. Visibility 8% Worn wiper blades, chipped windscreen, empty washer bottle Yes — wipers, washers and a look across the screen

About one car in four fails its MOT at the first attempt. DVSA test data put the final failure rate at 22.8% in 2024, and 27.2% of cars failed at the first attempt in 2025. The single most common defect of all is a tyre worn below the 1.6mm legal limit — it failed 759,464 cars in 2024 alone, according to analysis of DVSA test data. Tyres and brakes also dominate the most serious failures: defective tyres account for around 60% of all dangerous defects, and brakes for around 29%.

The failures you can prevent in 10 minutes

Most MOT failures are not expensive mechanical problems — they are small jobs the owner could have done on the driveway. Before you book the test, spend 10 minutes on these:

  • Every bulb. Switch on headlights, full beam, indicators, hazards, brake lights and the number plate light, then walk around the car. A blown bulb is the single most common lighting failure and usually costs a few pounds.
  • Wiper blades. Run the wipers with the washers on. If they smear, judder or leave lines, replace them — worn blades are one of the most recorded failure items in DVSA data.
  • Screenwash. Top up the washer bottle. An empty bottle is a major defect on its own, so the car fails for a job that takes a minute.
  • Tyre tread. Push a 20p coin into the main grooves. If you can see the coin's outer band, the tread is likely below the 1.6mm legal limit — and that is the most common single reason cars fail.
  • Number plate. Make sure the plate is clean, readable and properly lit. A plate lamp that does not work is a failure.

These five checks catch the faults behind a large share of all failures, and none of them needs tools or a garage visit.

Do older cars fail more often?

Yes — the older the car, the more likely it is to fail. Analysis of DVSA 2024 test data shows 85.2% of cars aged 3 to 5 pass first time, falling to 76.3% at 6 to 9 years, 63.7% at 10 to 14 years, and 56.8% at 15 to 20 years. What fails changes with age too: younger cars most often fail on tyres, visibility and brakes, while older cars fail most on lights and electrics, suspension and brakes. If your car is over 10 years old, the suspension and lighting checks above matter even more — and checking the car's MOT history before the test shows you exactly what it failed on before, so you can fix the same items first.

What happens if your car fails its MOT?

A fail is not the end of the story. Only the dangerous and major defects must be fixed before the car can pass — minor defects and advisories do not stop it passing. You can usually drive the car away if your old MOT is still in date and no dangerous defects were found, but if a dangerous defect was recorded, the car must not be driven at all. A partial retest is often free if you return to the same test centre within 10 working days, and it can never cost more than half the original test fee — no more than £27.43 for a car. Our guide to what to do if your car fails its MOT walks through every step, and our MOT cost guide has the full retest rules.

How MOT defects are graded

In May 2018, the DVSA restructured MOT defect categorisation to align with standardised European vehicle roadworthiness rules. Every defect discovered during an inspection falls strictly into one of four distinct tiers:

Advisory Items that are not currently defects but represent components showing signs of wear or early deterioration. An advisory does not prevent the vehicle from passing its MOT.
Minor Defects that have no significant effect on vehicle safety or the environment. The vehicle passes its MOT, but the registered keeper is advised to repair the issue promptly.
Major Defects that may affect the vehicle's safety, impact the environment, or put other road users at risk. A major defect results in an immediate MOT failure.
Dangerous Defects that constitute a direct and immediate risk to road safety or have a severe environmental impact. The vehicle fails immediately and must not be driven on public roads.

Crucially, if your car receives a Dangerous defect, you cannot legally drive it away from the test centre—even if your previous year's MOT certificate has days or weeks remaining before its calendar expiry. Driving a vehicle in a dangerous condition is a serious criminal offence carrying fines of up to £2,500 and 3 penalty points.

Primary inspection areas and common failure points

The annual MOT examination evaluates dozens of specific mechanical, structural, and electrical safety standards across eight primary vehicle systems:

1. Lamps, reflectors, and electrical equipment

Lighting defects remain among the most common failure items noted across UK testing stations. Testers verify the operation, position, colour, and alignment of headlamps (including dipped and main beams), front and rear sidelights, brake lamps, reversing lights, direction indicators, hazard warning switches, rear fog lamps, and registration plate illumination. Lenses must not be heavily clouded, cracked, or allowing white light to show where red or amber is required.

2. Tyres and wheels

Tyres must meet statutory road safety requirements. For passenger cars and light goods vehicles, the legal minimum tread depth is 1.6mm across the central three-quarters of the tyre profile around the entire circumference. Testers also examine tyre sidewalls for cuts, bulges, exposed cord or ply, uneven wear indicative of suspension misalignment, and correct sizing across each axle.

3. Brakes and braking performance

The braking system is evaluated dynamically on rolling road rollers to measure brake efficiency, balance between left and right wheels, and handbrake holding force. Physical checks assess brake pad thickness, disc scoring, rusted or leaking hydraulic lines, master cylinder fluid levels, and anti-lock braking system (ABS) warning lamps on the instrument cluster.

4. Steering and suspension

Testers raise the vehicle on a hydraulic ramp to stress-test suspension joints, coil springs, shock absorbers, wishbones, and anti-roll bar linkages. Corroded suspension springs, leaking hydraulic dampers, and worn steering rack ball joints with excessive play will cause an immediate test refusal.

5. Visibility, windscreen, and mirrors

The driver's field of vision must be free of significant obstructions. On the windscreen, damage or chips larger than 10mm directly within the swept wiper area in front of the driver (Zone A) or larger than 40mm across the remaining swept area will trigger a failure. Wiper blades must not be split or perish, and windscreen washer jets must provide sufficient fluid to clear the glass.

6. Exhaust emissions and environmental controls

Exhaust emissions are measured with a calibrated exhaust gas analyser. Petrol vehicles are checked for carbon monoxide (CO) and hydrocarbon limits, while diesel vehicles are evaluated for exhaust smoke density and particulate filter (DPF) integrity. Vehicles fitted with catalytic converters or DPFs from the factory will fail if these emissions devices have been removed or tampered with.

7. Body, chassis, and structural integrity

Corrosion or severe body damage within 30cm of safety-critical structural mounting points (such as suspension mounts, seatbelt anchors, and subframes) represents a major structural hazard. Additionally, all passenger doors must latch securely and open cleanly from both inside and outside.

8. Seatbelts and supplemental restraint systems (SRS)

All fitted seatbelts are checked for operation, condition, and retractability. Belts must latch securely, release smoothly, and have no cuts, fraying, or stitching damage. The airbag and SRS warning lights on the dashboard must illuminate during the ignition check and extinguish normally.

Practical pre-test checklist you can do at home

Carrying out basic preventative checks in your driveway before submitting your vehicle for inspection can prevent frustrating and unnecessary failures. Follow this straightforward 10-point checklist — and for the full version, see our pre-MOT checklist, which covers 15 checks in the order to do them.

  1. Check every light bulb: Turn on your hazard lights, headlights, and fog lights, and walk around the vehicle. Ask a friend or neighbour to stand behind the car while you press the brake pedal to confirm brake lights work.
  2. Top up windscreen washer fluid: An empty washer fluid bottle results in an immediate MOT failure, even if the wipers and nozzles operate perfectly.
  3. Inspect windscreen wiper blades: Run your finger along the rubber wiper blades. If they are torn, hardened, or leave large smears on the windscreen, replace them beforehand.
  4. Measure tyre tread depth: Use a 20p coin to check tread depth. Place the coin into the main grooves; if the outer band of the coin is visible, your tread is likely beneath the legal 1.6mm threshold.
  5. Check tyre pressures and sidewalls: Inflate tyres to the manufacturer's recommended pressures and inspect sidewalls for gouges or protruding bulges.
  6. Test the horn: Give the horn a firm press. It must emit a continuous, uniform, audible tone.
  7. Check all seatbelts: Pull each seatbelt out completely to verify there are no frays or tears, click it into its buckle, and give it a sharp tug to verify the inertia-reel locking mechanism engages.
  8. Clear windscreen obstructions: Remove phone holders, dashcam mounts, or hanging air fresheners that encroach into the swept area of the windscreen.
  9. Check dashboard warning lights: Turn the ignition on. Ensure the ABS, airbag, and engine management lights extinguish after the engine starts. A lit ABS or airbag light is an automatic failure. Our guide to dashboard warning lights that fail an MOT lists every light that matters.
  10. Ensure the vehicle is clean and tidy: Clear out the boot and footwells. Testers are legally entitled to refuse to inspect a vehicle if it is excessively dirty, filled with clutter, or contains hazardous items.

Quick answers

What is the most common reason cars fail their MOT?

Lights and electrics — about a quarter of all MOT failures involve lamps, reflectors or electrical equipment, usually just a blown bulb. The single most common individual fault is a tyre worn below the 1.6mm legal tread limit.

How many cars fail their MOT first time?

About one car in four. DVSA test data put the final failure rate at 22.8% in 2024, and 27.2% of cars failed at the first attempt in 2025.

Can I drive my car after it fails its MOT?

Only if your old MOT is still in date and no dangerous defects were found. If a dangerous defect was recorded, the car must not be driven at all until it is repaired. Our guide to what to do if your car fails its MOT explains your options.

Will advisories fail my MOT?

No. An advisory means the tester spotted wear or a problem that is not yet a defect. The car still passes, but you should get the item fixed before it becomes a failure at the next test. See our guide to MOT advisories explained.

Does an empty screenwash bottle fail an MOT?

Yes. The washers are a testable item, and no washer fluid is a major defect — so the car fails. Topping up the bottle takes a minute and costs very little.

How much does an MOT retest cost?

Often nothing. If the car stays at the test centre, or comes back by the end of the next working day for simple faults, the partial retest is free. Otherwise a partial retest within 10 working days costs at most half the original fee — no more than £27.43 for a car. See the full rules on our MOT cost page.
