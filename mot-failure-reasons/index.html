Common MOT failure reasons and how to avoid them

Last reviewed: October 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

Vehicles fail their MOT when an inspector identifies one or more faults classified as major or dangerous under official Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency standards. Many of the most frequent reasons for an inspection refusal are minor maintenance issues—such as blown lightbulbs or empty screenwash—that owners can readily identify and resolve before the test begins.

Understanding how test items are inspected and how defects are categorised enables motorists to save time and avoid re-inspection fees. Below, we break down the four official defect severity grades, examine the primary vehicle systems evaluated during the annual inspection, and provide an actionable DIY pre-test checklist.

How MOT defects are graded

In May 2018, the DVSA restructured MOT defect categorisation to align with standardised European vehicle roadworthiness rules. Every defect discovered during an inspection falls strictly into one of four distinct tiers:

Advisory Items that are not currently defects but represent components showing signs of wear or early deterioration. An advisory does not prevent the vehicle from passing its MOT.
Minor Defects that have no significant effect on vehicle safety or the environment. The vehicle passes its MOT, but the registered keeper is advised to repair the issue promptly.
Major Defects that may affect the vehicle's safety, impact the environment, or put other road users at risk. A major defect results in an immediate MOT failure.
Dangerous Defects that constitute a direct and immediate risk to road safety or have a severe environmental impact. The vehicle fails immediately and must not be driven on public roads.

Crucially, if your car receives a Dangerous defect, you cannot legally drive it away from the test centre—even if your previous year's MOT certificate has days or weeks remaining before its calendar expiry. Driving a vehicle in a dangerous condition is a serious criminal offence carrying fines of up to £2,500 and 3 penalty points.

Primary inspection areas and common failure points

The annual MOT examination evaluates dozens of specific mechanical, structural, and electrical safety standards across eight primary vehicle systems:

1. Lamps, reflectors, and electrical equipment

Lighting defects remain among the most common failure items noted across UK testing stations. Testers verify the operation, position, colour, and alignment of headlamps (including dipped and main beams), front and rear sidelights, brake lamps, reversing lights, direction indicators, hazard warning switches, rear fog lamps, and registration plate illumination. Lenses must not be heavily clouded, cracked, or allowing white light to show where red or amber is required.

2. Tyres and wheels

Tyres must meet statutory road safety requirements. For passenger cars and light goods vehicles, the legal minimum tread depth is 1.6mm across the central three-quarters of the tyre profile around the entire circumference. Testers also examine tyre sidewalls for cuts, bulges, exposed cord or ply, uneven wear indicative of suspension misalignment, and correct sizing across each axle.

3. Brakes and braking performance

The braking system is evaluated dynamically on rolling road rollers to measure brake efficiency, balance between left and right wheels, and handbrake holding force. Physical checks assess brake pad thickness, disc scoring, rusted or leaking hydraulic lines, master cylinder fluid levels, and anti-lock braking system (ABS) warning lamps on the instrument cluster.

4. Steering and suspension

Testers raise the vehicle on a hydraulic ramp to stress-test suspension joints, coil springs, shock absorbers, wishbones, and anti-roll bar linkages. Corroded suspension springs, leaking hydraulic dampers, and worn steering rack ball joints with excessive play will cause an immediate test refusal.

5. Visibility, windscreen, and mirrors

The driver's field of vision must be free of significant obstructions. On the windscreen, damage or chips larger than 10mm directly within the swept wiper area in front of the driver (Zone A) or larger than 40mm across the remaining swept area will trigger a failure. Wiper blades must not be split or perish, and windscreen washer jets must provide sufficient fluid to clear the glass.

6. Exhaust emissions and environmental controls

Exhaust emissions are measured with a calibrated exhaust gas analyser. Petrol vehicles are checked for carbon monoxide (CO) and hydrocarbon limits, while diesel vehicles are evaluated for exhaust smoke density and particulate filter (DPF) integrity. Vehicles fitted with catalytic converters or DPFs from the factory will fail if these emissions devices have been removed or tampered with.

7. Body, chassis, and structural integrity

Corrosion or severe body damage within 30cm of safety-critical structural mounting points (such as suspension mounts, seatbelt anchors, and subframes) represents a major structural hazard. Additionally, all passenger doors must latch securely and open cleanly from both inside and outside.

8. Seatbelts and supplemental restraint systems (SRS)

All fitted seatbelts are checked for operation, condition, and retractability. Belts must latch securely, release smoothly, and have no cuts, fraying, or stitching damage. The airbag and SRS warning lights on the dashboard must illuminate during the ignition check and extinguish normally.

Practical pre-test checklist you can do at home

Carrying out basic preventative checks in your driveway before submitting your vehicle for inspection can prevent frustrating and unnecessary failures. Follow this straightforward 10-point checklist:

  1. Check every light bulb: Turn on your hazard lights, headlights, and fog lights, and walk around the vehicle. Ask a friend or neighbour to stand behind the car while you press the brake pedal to confirm brake lights work.
  2. Top up windscreen washer fluid: An empty washer fluid bottle results in an immediate MOT failure, even if the wipers and nozzles operate perfectly.
  3. Inspect windscreen wiper blades: Run your finger along the rubber wiper blades. If they are torn, hardened, or leave large smears on the windscreen, replace them beforehand.
  4. Measure tyre tread depth: Use a 20p coin to check tread depth. Place the coin into the main grooves; if the outer band of the coin is visible, your tread is likely beneath the legal 1.6mm threshold.
  5. Check tyre pressures and sidewalls: Inflate tyres to the manufacturer's recommended pressures and inspect sidewalls for gouges or protruding bulges.
  6. Test the horn: Give the horn a firm press. It must emit a continuous, uniform, audible tone.
  7. Check all seatbelts: Pull each seatbelt out completely to verify there are no frays or tears, click it into its buckle, and give it a sharp tug to verify the inertia-reel locking mechanism engages.
  8. Clear windscreen obstructions: Remove phone holders, dashcam mounts, or hanging air fresheners that encroach into the swept area of the windscreen.
  9. Check dashboard warning lights: Turn the ignition on. Ensure the ABS, airbag, and engine management lights extinguish after the engine starts. A lit ABS or airbag light is an automatic failure.
  10. Ensure the vehicle is clean and tidy: Clear out the boot and footwells. Testers are legally entitled to refuse to inspect a vehicle if it is excessively dirty, filled with clutter, or contains hazardous items.
