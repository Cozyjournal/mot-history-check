MOT Advisories Explained

Written by MOT History Check editorial team Last updated: October 2024 DVSA Defect Categorisation Guide

An MOT advisory is an official observation noted on a vehicle's test certificate indicating that a component has started to wear out or show early signs of damage, but has not yet deteriorated enough to fail statutory safety standards. Advisories do not fail the MOT, and the vehicle remains completely road-legal; however, the component will likely require maintenance before the subsequent annual test.

Defect Categories: Advisory vs Minor vs Major vs Dangerous

Under the DVSA testing framework updated in May 2018 (aligned with EU Directive 2014/45/EU), faults are split into 4 distinct classifications. Understanding these categories determines whether your vehicle has legally passed and whether you can drive it away from the testing bay.

Defect Category MOT Result Legal Driving Status Required Action
Advisory PASS Legal to drive Monitor condition regularly; plan for replacement at next service or within 3–6 months.
Minor PASS Legal to drive Repair as soon as possible. Minor safety issue with no immediate danger to vehicle or road users.
Major FAIL Illegal to drive* Repair immediately. May compromise safety, impact the environment, or put other road users at risk.
Dangerous FAIL Strictly Illegal to drive Do not drive. Causes direct and immediate risk to road safety or severe environmental harm.

*Legal exception: If a car fails with a Major defect and its previous MOT certificate is still valid, you may only drive it away if the fault does not render the vehicle unroadworthy under the Road Traffic Act. For Dangerous defects, driving is strictly illegal in all circumstances.

Common MOT Advisories & What They Actually Mean

Testers use standardised DVSA phraseology. Here is what those technical notes translate to in plain English:

"Brake pad(s) wearing thin"

The plain English: The tester can see the friction lining on your brake pads has worn down to approximately 2.0mm to 3.0mm. The legal failure limit is 1.5mm.

What to do: Depending on your annual mileage, you have between 1,000 and 3,000 miles before metal-on-metal contact scores your expensive brake discs. Book a brake pad replacement within 4 to 8 weeks.

"Nearside/Offside Tyre worn close to legal limit"

The plain English: "Nearside" refers to the passenger side (closest to the kerb); "Offside" is the driver's side. The tyre tread is between 1.6mm and 2.5mm (legal minimum is 1.6mm).

What to do: Wet-weather stopping distances increase drastically when tread dips below 3mm due to reduced aquaplaning resistance. Replace this tyre promptly, especially before autumn and winter driving.

"Suspension arm pin or bush worn but not resulting in excessive movement"

The plain English: The rubber bush dampening vibrations between the metal suspension arm and the chassis has started cracking or perishing with age, but there is no dangerous play yet.

What to do: This will inevitably become an MOT failure within 12 to 24 months as road vibrations worsen the tear. Budget to replace the suspension bush or wishbone assembly before next year's test.

"Slight oil leak / fluid misting on shock absorber"

The plain English: A tiny film of hydraulic damper fluid or engine oil was observed on the casing. Because it is not actively dripping to the ground, it passes as an advisory.

What to do: Clean the area with brake cleaner and inspect again in a month. If oil continues weeping, the internal seal has failed and the shock absorber must be renewed.

"Brake pipe slightly corroded / surface rust"

The plain English: The protective plastic coating on steel hydraulic brake lines has peeled, and mild surface oxidation has begun. If scraped, the pipe metal remains intact.

What to do: Clean off the surface rust and coat the pipe in rust-inhibiting grease or waxoyl. If left exposed to road salt, pitting rust will lead to a brake fluid blowout and immediate test fail.

Should You Fix MOT Advisories Immediately?

Not all advisories require immediate emergency spending. Follow this practical decision framework:

Items to Fix Promptly (1–2 Months)

  • Brake pads or shoes: Wearing fast; delaying will ruin the brake discs and double your repair bill.
  • Tyres under 2.5mm: Severely compromised wet braking; dangerous in rainy UK conditions.
  • Play in wheel bearings: Can deteriorate rapidly and risk wheel seizure at motorway speeds.

Items That Can Wait for Next Service

  • Surface rust on subframe/chassis: Slow-moving oxidation that can be treated at your next annual service.
  • Exhaust bracket corrosion: Non-critical as long as secondary hangers hold the exhaust pipe securely.
  • Minor windscreen chips: Outside driver view that have already stabilized.

Do Advisories Affect a Car's Resale Value?

Yes, substantially. Because every prospective buyer can look up a vehicle's MOT history on GOV.UK in seconds, an MOT pass littered with 5 or 6 advisories provides instant leverage to haggle down the asking price.

According to motor trade industry surveys:

  • A "clean" MOT pass (with zero advisories) commands between £300 and £600 more on private sales compared to the same vehicle carrying multiple advisories.
  • Buyers often perceive advisories as evidence of a neglected car where the owner avoided routine preventative maintenance.
  • If selling your car, investing £120 in new brake pads or a fresh tyre before listing it cleans the MOT history record and recovers its cost during negotiations.
