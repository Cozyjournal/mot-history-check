MOT advisories explained: what they mean and what to fix

Last reviewed: November 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

No — an MOT advisory is not a fail, and it does not make your car illegal to drive. It is the tester's way of saying "this part is wearing out, keep an eye on it." Your car passed, your certificate is valid for the full year, and there is no legal deadline for fixing an advisory. But ignoring the wrong one can turn a cheap fix into a failed MOT next year, or worse. This guide explains what each type of advisory means, which ones to fix first, how to read the tester's wording, and how to use advisories when buying a used car.

When reviewing your test summary sheet or checking a prospective used car online, interpreting advisory notes correctly can save you hundreds of pounds in urgent garage bills. Here is how advisories differ from formal minor defects, how to evaluate which repairs are urgent, and how to use recurring advisories to your advantage during car purchasing negotiations.

What is an MOT advisory notice?

During an annual inspection, an authorized MOT technician evaluates vehicle assemblies against strict minimum safety tolerances defined in the Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency (DVSA) inspection manual. If a part passes the required functional test but exhibits early corrosion, slight mechanical play, or approaching tread depth limits, the tester records an advisory notice — the VT32.

Advisories are recorded digitally on the central DVSA database and appear permanently on the vehicle's public test history. Common examples include:

  • Tyres worn close to the legal limit: E.g., "Front tyre worn close to legal limit, measuring 2mm." (The legal minimum is 1.6mm).
  • Brake friction material wear: E.g., "Rear brake pads wearing thin."
  • Minor suspension joint play: E.g., "Nearside front track rod end ball joint has slight play."
  • Surface corrosion: E.g., "Rear subframe has light surface corrosion."
  • Undertrays or covers fitted: Testers frequently note that engine undertrays or plastic sill covers prevented complete inspection of certain lines or structures.

Advisory, minor, major, dangerous: the four results

Since 20 May 2018, testers grade every defect as dangerous, major or minor. Advisories sit alongside those grades — they are the tester's own early warning, not a defect grade. GOV.UK puts it simply: your car passes if the result lists only minor problems or advisories, and fails if it lists dangerous or major problems.

Result What it means Pass or fail? Do you have to fix it?
Advisory A part is wearing out but still meets the standard today. The tester expects it to need work before the next test. Pass No deadline. Fix it before it gets worse.
Minor A real fault, but one with no significant effect on safety — for example a wiper that smears or a number plate lamp that has failed while another still works. Pass As soon as possible. It is already a fault, not a prediction.
Major A fault that could affect safety or the environment — for example a tyre below the 1.6mm limit or a failed headlamp. Fail Yes, then the car must be retested. See our guide on what to do if your car fails its MOT.
Dangerous A direct and immediate risk to road safety — for example a brake pipe about to burst or a tyre with cord showing. Fail Yes, and you must not drive the car until it is fixed.

Tests carried out before May 2018 use older wording — "pass with advisories" and "fail" — but the meaning is the same, and old results stay on the car's history in their original words.

How advisories differ from "Minor" defects

Many drivers confuse minor defects with advisories because neither will fail the vehicle. Since the 2018 change, defects are graded dangerous, major or minor, and advisories sit alongside those grades as the tester's own notes.

However, there is an important distinction:

  • Minor defects: These are formal, predefined defects in the MOT testing manual (for example, a malfunctioning registration plate lamp where another bulb still functions, or slightly degraded wiper blades). They represent faults that have already occurred, but their severity is insufficient to constitute a road hazard. The car passes, but the defect must be repaired as soon as possible.
  • Advisories: These are discretionary observations logged by the technician. They describe items that are currently roadworthy and within legal tolerances on the day of the test, but which are anticipated to deteriorate in the near future.

How to read the tester's wording

Advisory notes are written in the tester's shorthand, and the same phrases appear on thousands of certificates. Once you know the code, they are easy to read.

Which side is which. Testers say nearside and offside instead of left and right. On a UK car, nearside is the passenger side (the side nearest the kerb, on the left) and offside is the driver's side (on the right). So "nearside front tyre worn close to legal limit" means the front-left tyre.

The phrases you will see most often:

  • "Worn close to the legal limit" — the part is nearly at the point where it would fail. For tyres, that means the tread is approaching 1.6mm. Act on this one soon.
  • "Slight play" — a joint or bush moves a little more than it should, but not enough to fail. "Slight play in the nearside front track rod end ball joint" is one of the most common advisories of all.
  • "Corrosion, but not excessive" — there is rust, but it has not weakened the part yet. Worth watching; on older cars it is the advisory most likely to become a fail.
  • "Monitor and repair if necessary" — the tester's standard wording for "keep an eye on this, fix it when it gets worse."
  • "Under-trays fitted" / "covers fitted" — the tester could not fully inspect something because a plastic undertray or sill cover was in the way. This is a note about the test, not a fault in your car.
  • "Items removed from driver's view" — something like a sat-nav mount or air freshener was blocking the windscreen, and the tester moved it. Not a fault.

You may also see a number in brackets after a note, such as (5.2.3). That is the section number in the DVSA MOT inspection manual the tester was checking against — it is there for the record, and you do not need to look it up.

How to decide what to fix and when

Because advisories do not legally invalidate your MOT certificate, you are not mandated to repair them immediately to keep your car on the road. However, ignoring them entirely can lead to sudden roadside breakdowns, increased fuel consumption, or dangerous failures months later.

To prioritize repairs sensibly, categorize your advisories into three distinct urgency groups:

1. High priority: Fast-wearing safety components

Brake pads, brake discs, and tyres should be addressed promptly. If a tyre is flagged at 2mm, it may reach the illegal 1.6mm threshold within a few thousand miles of routine commuting. Similarly, worn brake pads can quickly wear down to their metal backing plates, damaging expensive brake discs and compromising emergency stopping distances.

2. Medium priority: Steering and suspension joints

Advisories noting slight play in ball joints, anti-roll bar drop links, or suspension arm bushes can generally wait for your vehicle's next scheduled service, provided you do not notice severe knocking, steering vibration, or abnormal tyre wear. Have your mechanic inspect these items at intermediate oil change intervals.

3. Low priority: Surface corrosion and aesthetic items

Light surface rust on structural components or exhaust heat shields is common on UK vehicles exposed to winter road salt. These items can often be treated with rust inhibitor or monitored at future inspections before replacement becomes structurally necessary.

The most common advisories — and what they cost to fix

Advisories cluster around a few wearing parts. The prices below are rough typical ranges from UK independent garages in 2026 — they vary a lot by car, part quality and where you live, so always get a quote before agreeing to work. The MOT test fee itself is capped by law; see our MOT cost guide for the maximum fees.

Advisory What it usually means Typical cost to fix
Tyre worn close to the legal limit Tread is approaching 1.6mm, or one edge is wearing faster than the rest. £60–£150 per tyre, fitted
Brake pads wearing thin The friction material is nearly gone. Left alone, the pads wear to metal and damage the discs. £80–£200 per axle
Brake discs worn or corroded Discs are scored, thin or rusted. Usually replaced together with the pads. £200–£400 per axle, with pads
Slight play in a suspension joint or bush A ball joint, track rod end or bush has a little movement. Often just the one part, not the whole arm. £100–£300
Exhaust deteriorating or leaking slightly A section of pipe, silencer or mounting is starting to fail. £100–£400, depending on the section
Surface corrosion, not excessive Rust on a subframe, sill or brake pipe that has not weakened the part yet. £0 to monitor; £100+ if treated or repaired
Wiper blade or washer fault A blade that smears or a washer jet that does not clear the screen. £10–£30 for blades

The pattern is the same in every case: fixing an advisory costs a fraction of what the same part costs once it fails — a £100 set of brake pads becomes a £300 discs-and-pads job, and a £60 tyre becomes a failed MOT plus a retest.

Do advisories expire or get removed?

No. An advisory never expires, and fixing the part does not remove it from the record. Every advisory a tester records stays permanently on the car's MOT history, which anyone can look up free on GOV.UK with the registration number.

What changes is the next test. If you fix the part, the next MOT simply will not list that advisory again — the old note stays visible in the history of the earlier test, where it belongs. That is normal, and it is not a black mark: a history that shows an advisory one year and no mention of it the next is exactly what a good maintenance record looks like.

The reverse is also true. If an advisory appears year after year, unchanged, it means nobody fixed it — and that is the pattern buyers look for (see below).

What happens if you ignore an advisory?

Nothing happens straight away. There is no fine for having an advisory, no deadline, and no one will chase you about it. But two things can catch you out.

It can become a failure. That is what advisories are for: the part is on its way to failing. A tyre at 2mm of tread will be below the 1.6mm legal limit within a few thousand miles, and a brake pad flagged as thin will wear to metal. At the next MOT the same item comes back as a major defect — a fail, plus a repair bill and a retest.

Your car must stay roadworthy at all times. An MOT certificate only proves the car met the standard on the day of the test. GOV.UK is clear that a vehicle must meet the minimum standards of roadworthiness at all times, and driving a vehicle in a dangerous condition can lead to a fine of up to £2,500, a driving ban and 3 penalty points. An advisory is written evidence that you were warned about the part — so if it deteriorates into something dangerous and you keep driving, you cannot say you did not know. Your insurer also expects the car to be roadworthy at all times, so an advisory left until the part becomes dangerous can complicate a claim if something goes wrong.

Can you sell a car with advisories?

Yes. Advisories do not stop a sale, and there is no rule that you must fix them before selling. What they do affect is the price: a buyer who checks the MOT history will see them and use them to negotiate, just as you would. The honest approach is to fix the cheap safety items (tyres, pads) and be upfront about the rest — a car with advisories that have been dealt with sells more easily than one where the same advisory has sat there for three years.

One thing to get right: describe the car accurately. If you tell a buyer the car "needs nothing" while its history shows an advisory for worn brakes, that is a misdescription, and a private buyer can come back to you over it. Let the history speak for itself, and price the car with it in mind. Our guide to checking MOT history when buying a used car shows the same checks from the buyer's side.

Using repeated advisories when buying a used car

When shopping for a second-hand car, the MOT history is one of your most powerful investigative tools. By examining the chronology of past tests on the DVSA database, you can immediately identify how diligently the previous keeper looked after the vehicle.

Watch out for the following red flags in the advisory records:

  • The same advisory repeated year after year: If a car receives an advisory for "offside front suspension arm pin or bush worn" in 2023, 2024, and 2025 without ever being fixed, it proves the seller only carried out the bare minimum required to get a pass certificate and neglected preventative maintenance.
  • Advisory disappearing without explanation: If a major suspension advisory disappears the following year, check whether the seller has garage receipts proving the part was replaced, or whether the car was simply taken to a more lenient testing station.
  • Sudden accumulation of multiple advisories: A vehicle that transitions from zero advisories to seven advisories in a single test indicates that wear-and-tear components across all corners are nearing the end of their operational lifespan, signalling a large impending maintenance bill.

You can use outstanding advisories directly to negotiate a fair discount on the asking price. Request quotes from an independent garage for repairing the flagged items and ask the seller to either rectify the faults before sale or deduct that estimate from the purchase price.

Quick answers

Does an MOT advisory mean my car failed?

No. An advisory means your car passed. It is a note that a part is wearing out and may need work before the next test — but on the day of the test, the car met the standard.

Do MOT advisories expire?

No. An advisory has no expiry date and never needs renewing. It stays permanently on the car's MOT history, even after the part is fixed. A later test simply will not list it again once the work is done.

Do I have to fix an advisory before driving?

No, there is no legal deadline for fixing an advisory. But your car must stay roadworthy at all times, so a safety item like tyres or brakes should be fixed promptly — if it wears into a dangerous condition, driving it can lead to a fine of up to £2,500.

Will an advisory make my car fail its next MOT?

It can. An advisory is a warning that the part is heading for failure. If it wears past the limit before the next test — a tyre below 1.6mm, a brake pad worn to metal — the same item comes back as a major defect and the car fails.

What does "nearside" and "offside" mean on an MOT?

Nearside is the passenger side (left on a UK car, nearest the kerb) and offside is the driver's side (right). So "nearside front tyre" means the front-left tyre.

If I fix the part, does the advisory disappear from the history?

No. The advisory stays on the record of that test forever. The next MOT will not list it again if the part is fixed — and that is what a good maintenance history looks like.

Can I sell a car with MOT advisories?

Yes. Advisories do not stop a sale. Buyers will use them to negotiate the price, so fix the cheap safety items first and describe the car honestly.

Can I challenge or appeal an advisory?

Talk to the test centre first — many advisories are a judgement call, and a good tester will explain what they saw. A formal DVSA appeal covers a pass or fail decision, not an advisory note on its own, so an advisory alone is not something you can appeal to the DVSA.

What is the difference between an advisory and a minor defect?

A minor defect is a real fault that has already happened, but is not serious enough to fail the car — it should be repaired as soon as possible. An advisory is not a fault yet: the part still meets the standard, but the tester expects it to need work soon. Both mean the car passes.

Where can I see a car's advisories?

On the free GOV.UK MOT history service, using the registration number. It shows every test, including advisories, failures and mileage, for cars tested in England, Scotland and Wales.
