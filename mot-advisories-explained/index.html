MOT advisories explained: what they mean and what to fix

Last reviewed: October 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

An MOT advisory is an official observation logged by a tester alerting you that a vehicle component is beginning to wear out but still satisfies minimum legal safety standards. Advisories do not prevent a car from passing its annual test, but they indicate maintenance items that will likely require attention before the next inspection.

When reviewing your test summary sheet or checking a prospective used car online, interpreting advisory notes correctly can save you hundreds of pounds in urgent garage bills. Here is how advisories differ from formal minor defects, how to evaluate which repairs are urgent, and how to use recurring advisories to your advantage during car purchasing negotiations.

What is an MOT advisory notice?

During an annual inspection, an authorized MOT technician evaluates vehicle assemblies against strict minimum safety tolerances defined in the Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency (DVSA) inspection manual. If a part passes the required functional test but exhibits early corrosion, slight mechanical play, or approaching tread depth limits, the tester records an advisory notice.

Advisories are recorded digitally on the central DVSA database and appear permanently on the vehicle's public test history. Common examples include:

  • Tyres worn close to the legal limit: E.g., "Front tyre worn close to legal limit, measuring 2mm." (The legal minimum is 1.6mm).
  • Brake friction material wear: E.g., "Rear brake pads wearing thin."
  • Minor suspension joint play: E.g., "Nearside front track rod end ball joint has slight play."
  • Surface corrosion: E.g., "Rear subframe has light surface corrosion."
  • Undertrays or covers fitted: Testers frequently note that engine undertrays or plastic sill covers prevented complete inspection of certain lines or structures.

How advisories differ from "Minor" defects

Since the regulatory overhaul in 2018, vehicle defects have been categorized into four official bands: Minor, Major, Dangerous, and Advisory. Many drivers confuse minor defects with advisories because neither will fail the vehicle.

However, there is an important distinction:

  • Minor defects: These are formal, predefined defects in the MOT testing manual (for example, a malfunctioning registration plate lamp where another bulb still functions, or slightly degraded wiper blades). They represent faults that have already occurred, but their severity is insufficient to constitute a road hazard. The car passes, but the defect must be repaired as soon as possible.
  • Advisories: These are discretionary observations logged by the technician. They describe items that are currently roadworthy and within legal tolerances on the day of the test, but which are anticipated to deteriorate in the near future.

How to decide what to fix and when

Because advisories do not legally invalidate your MOT certificate, you are not mandated to repair them immediately to keep your car on the road. However, ignoring them entirely can lead to sudden roadside breakdowns, increased fuel consumption, or dangerous failures months later.

To prioritize repairs sensibly, categorize your advisories into three distinct urgency groups:

1. High priority: Fast-wearing safety components

Brake pads, brake discs, and tyres should be addressed promptly. If a tyre is flagged at 2mm, it may reach the illegal 1.6mm threshold within a few thousand miles of routine commuting. Similarly, worn brake pads can quickly wear down to their metal backing plates, damaging expensive brake discs and compromising emergency stopping distances.

2. Medium priority: Steering and suspension joints

Advisories noting slight play in ball joints, anti-roll bar drop links, or suspension arm bushes can generally wait for your vehicle's next scheduled service, provided you do not notice severe knocking, steering vibration, or abnormal tyre wear. Have your mechanic inspect these items at intermediate oil change intervals.

3. Low priority: Surface corrosion and aesthetic items

Light surface rust on structural components or exhaust heat shields is common on UK vehicles exposed to winter road salt. These items can often be treated with rust inhibitor or monitored at future inspections before replacement becomes structurally necessary.

Using repeated advisories when buying a used car

When shopping for a second-hand car, the MOT history is one of your most powerful investigative tools. By examining the chronology of past tests on the DVSA database, you can immediately identify how diligently the previous keeper looked after the vehicle.

Watch out for the following red flags in the advisory records:

  • The same advisory repeated year after year: If a car receives an advisory for "offside front suspension arm pin or bush worn" in 2023, 2024, and 2025 without ever being fixed, it proves the seller only carried out the bare minimum required to get a pass certificate and neglected preventative maintenance.
  • Advisory disappearing without explanation: If a major suspension advisory disappears the following year, check whether the seller has garage receipts proving the part was replaced, or whether the car was simply taken to a more lenient testing station.
  • Sudden accumulation of multiple advisories: A vehicle that transitions from zero advisories to seven advisories in a single test indicates that wear-and-tear components across all corners are nearing the end of their operational lifespan, signalling a large impending maintenance bill.

You can use outstanding advisories directly to negotiate a fair discount on the asking price. Request quotes from an independent garage for repairing the flagged items and ask the seller to either rectify the faults before sale or deduct that estimate from the purchase price.
