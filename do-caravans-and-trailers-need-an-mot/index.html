Do Caravans and Trailers Need an MOT? The Rules Explained

Last reviewed: November 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

No — caravans do not need an MOT, and neither do ordinary trailers. The MOT is a test for motor vehicles, and a caravan is a trailer: it has no engine, so there is no MOT class for it, no test station that can issue a certificate for it, and no record to look up. But that is not the whole story. Motorhomes do need an MOT, heavy commercial trailers need an annual test instead, and every trailer on the road — tested or not — must be roadworthy. This guide explains where each type stands.

Do caravans need an MOT?

No. A touring caravan towed behind a car is not part of the MOT scheme at all, whatever its weight or age. The law that sets out which vehicles need a periodic test — the Road Traffic Act 1988 and the Motor Vehicles (Tests) Regulations 1981 — covers motor vehicles, and a caravan is not one. There is no MOT class for a caravan, no certificate to get, and nothing to renew each year.

That does not mean you can tow any old caravan. Every trailer on a public road must be in a roadworthy condition under the Construction and Use Regulations — tyres, brakes where fitted, lights, and the towing coupling all have to work. A caravan with bald tyres, a broken light cluster or a seized coupling is illegal to tow, MOT or no MOT. If you are buying a used caravan, our guide to checking a used vehicle’s MOT history still matters — because the car you tow it with has a test record worth reading before you spend money.

What about motorhomes and campervans?

Motorhomes are different, because they drive themselves. A motorhome or motor caravan is a motor vehicle in its own right, so it needs an MOT once it is three years old, just like a car. A motor caravan up to 3,000kg is tested as a Class 4 vehicle; heavier motorhomes fall into a higher class. Our guide to MOTs for motorhomes and campervans covers the classes, the costs and exactly what gets checked — including the point at which a big motorhome becomes a Class 7 or Class 5 test.

The key distinction is simple: if it has its own engine, it needs a test. If it is towed, it does not — unless it falls into the heavy-trailer rules below.

Which trailers DO need a test?

Heavy trailers do not get a car-style MOT. They get an annual test — the same test lorries and buses get — and it is booked at an authorised testing facility (ATF), not at a car MOT centre. GOV.UK says you need an annual test for:

Semi-trailers and vehicles built or adapted to form part of an articulated vehicle.

Horseboxes with a gross weight of more than 3,500kg.

‘A’ frame trailers and converter dollies manufactured on or after 1 January 1979.

Trailers with an unladen weight of more than 1,020kg with powered braking systems — that is, brakes powered by something other than the standard overrun brake, as well as the required parking brake.

Goods vehicles with a gross weight of more than 3,500kg — the lorry or tractor unit itself, tested as an HGV.

Trailers that need the test are usually first tested one year after they were first sold or supplied, and then every year after that. The trailer carries its own identification number, and its test history sits in the DVSA commercial-vehicle system and on the trailer’s plating certificate — not in the car MOT database, so you cannot look it up with a number plate the way you can a car. When you buy a heavy trailer, ask for the test certificate and check the next due date.

By contrast, a trailer with a maximum mass of 3,500kg or less is not tested annually — that covers almost every box trailer, plant trailer, car transporter and horse trailer towed behind a car. If you tow one of those, there is no test to book and no certificate to keep.

Small trailers: no test, but still rules

Being exempt from testing is not the same as being exempt from the law. Before every trip, check the trailer the way the DVSA expects:

Tyres. Look for cracks, bulges and tread depth — and remember that trailer tyres age even when they are barely used. Caravan tyres are generally recommended for replacement every five years, because age-related cracking is the usual failure.

Lights and indicators. Brake lights, indicators and the number plate light all have to work, and the trailer must display the same number plate as the towing vehicle.

Brakes and coupling. Overrun brakes need to move freely, the coupling must lock securely onto the tow ball, and the breakaway cable must be attached.

Weight. Stay inside the trailer’s maximum weight and your car’s towing limit, and check your driving licence entitlement for heavier combinations — the rules on what you can tow changed in late 2021, and most licence holders can now tow more than before, but your own entitlement is still the limit.

Trailers made on or after 29 October 2012 also need type approval before they can be sold or used on the road — that is the manufacturer’s or importer’s job, not a test you book, but it is why a new trailer comes with an approval certificate.

The tow car still needs its MOT

Whatever the trailer’s position, the vehicle doing the towing needs a valid MOT, tax and insurance, just like any other journey. A caravan holiday is a bad time to discover your car’s MOT ran out last week — check the expiry date before you set off, and if the test is due soon, our guide to how to book an MOT test walks through finding a centre and picking a date that keeps your renewal date. If you are buying the tow car second-hand, check its MOT history on GOV.UK first: past failures on brakes, suspension and tyres tell you more about a tow car than a shiny paint job does.

Taking a caravan or trailer abroad

No MOT does not mean no paperwork. If your trailer or caravan weighs over 750kg, you must register it with the DVLA before driving to or through most countries in Europe, apart from Ireland, Cyprus, Malta and Spain. Registration is done online through GOV.UK’s “Register your trailer to take it abroad” service: first registration costs £26, and the trailer gets its own registration plate — separate from the towing vehicle’s plate — and a trailer registration certificate to show a foreign authority if asked. A new registration certificate for a new keeper costs £21, and a replacement is £10.

You do not need to register a trailer that is only used in the UK, or only for journeys between the UK and Ireland. But if you tow through a country where the rule applies on the way to Cyprus, Malta or Spain, registration is still required. This is separate from the CRiS registration scheme for caravans, which is a security and identification register often required by insurers — registering with CRiS does not replace DVLA registration for travel abroad.

Caravan servicing: the voluntary check worth having

Although no test is required, most caravan owners still have an annual service — and it is worth doing. The National Caravan Council runs an Approved Workshop Scheme: independently audited service centres that carry out an annual habitation and chassis inspection, typically covering around 60 points, from damp and gas safety to the running gear underneath. It is not a legal requirement, and it is not an MOT, but it is the closest thing a caravan has to one — and it is what insurers and buyers look for when they ask whether the caravan has been looked after.

Quick answers

Do caravans need an MOT in the UK?

No. A towed touring caravan does not need an MOT, because it is a trailer, not a motor vehicle — there is no MOT class for it and no test station can issue a certificate for one. The caravan must still be roadworthy, and the car towing it must have a valid MOT.

Do trailers need an MOT?

Most ordinary trailers towed by a car do not need a test. But heavy trailers do: semi-trailers, horseboxes over 3,500kg gross weight, A-frame trailers and converter dollies made on or after 1 January 1979, and trailers over 1,020kg unladen weight with powered braking systems need an annual test at an authorised testing facility.

Do motorhomes need an MOT?

Yes. A motorhome is a motor vehicle in its own right, so it needs an MOT once it is three years old — tested as a Class 4 vehicle up to 3,500kg, and in a higher class if it is heavier.

Can I check a trailer’s MOT history online?

Not for an ordinary car trailer or caravan — there is no test and no record to look up. Heavy trailers that are tested annually do have a test history, but it sits in the DVSA commercial-vehicle system and on the trailer’s plating certificate, not in the car MOT database. What you can always check online is the towing vehicle’s MOT history.

Do I need to register my caravan to take it abroad?

If your trailer or caravan weighs over 750kg, you must register it with the DVLA before driving to or through most countries in Europe, apart from Ireland, Cyprus, Malta and Spain. First registration costs £26, and the trailer gets its own registration plate and certificate.

So: caravans and ordinary trailers need no MOT, motorhomes do, and heavy trailers get an annual test instead. The rule to remember is the one that never changes — tested or not, the trailer and the car towing it must both be roadworthy every time they go on the road.
