How to Check MOT History When Buying a Used Car

Last reviewed: October 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

A used car can look perfect on the driveway and still hide a troubled past. The quickest way to see that past is the car's MOT history, and it costs nothing. Every test the car has taken since 2005 is recorded by DVSA, and anyone can read the record with just the number plate. Do this check before you view the car, and again before you hand over any money.

How to check the MOT history for free

The official check takes about a minute:

  1. Ask the seller for the car's registration number, if it is not in the advert.
  2. Go to the MOT history service on GOV.UK.
  3. Type in the number plate and confirm the make and model shown are the car you are looking at.
  4. Read through the test results, starting with the most recent.

You will see whether each test was a pass or a fail, the mileage recorded at every test, the reasons for any failures, and any advisory notes the tester left. Our step-by-step guide on how to check MOT history walks through each screen if you want more detail. If you also want to see where each test was carried out, you will need the 11-digit reference number from the car's V5C log book, which the seller should have.

Red flags to look for

A long history is not a bad history. Plenty of good cars have failed an MOT at some point. What matters is the pattern. Watch out for these warning signs.

Mileage that goes down or jumps around

The mileage should climb steadily from test to test, roughly in line with how much the car is used. If one year's reading is lower than the year before, the odometer may have been wound back, which is known as clocking. Big unexplained jumps are also worth a question — a car that did 4,000 miles a year and then suddenly 30,000 may have had a harder life than the seller describes. A single odd reading can be a tester typing error, but several odd readings are a reason to walk away.

The same failure, year after year

One failure for a worn tyre is normal wear and tear. The same suspension or brake fault failing at test after test suggests the repair was done cheaply, or not done properly at all. Repeated failures in the same area point to a car that has been run on a tight budget, and you may inherit the bill. The most common patterns are covered in our guide to MOT failure reasons.

Advisories that never get fixed

Advisories are faults the tester noticed that were not bad enough to fail the car on the day — things like a tyre getting close to the legal limit or light corrosion starting. Good owners fix them soon after. If the same advisory appears two or three years running, it was ignored, and it may now be close to a failure. Read our guide to MOT advisories explained so you know which ones are cheap fixes and which ones cost real money.

Long gaps in the record

Cars need an MOT every year once they are three years old. A gap of 18 months or more between tests usually means the car sat unused, was off the road, or was driven without an MOT. None of those is automatically a deal-breaker, but a car that sat idle for a year can need new brakes, tyres and seals. Ask the seller to explain the gap.

A very recent pass after a long fail

Sometimes a car fails badly, disappears, then passes at a different test centre shortly after. That can be a genuine repair job done well. It can also be a quick patch-up to get a pass certificate for the sale. Compare the failure list with what the seller says was fixed, and check the work looks fresh when you view the car.

Questions to ask the seller

  • Can I see the V5C log book, and does the VIN on it match the car?
  • Have you kept service receipts or invoices for repairs after failed tests?
  • Why is there a gap in the MOT record in that year?
  • Has the car ever been in an accident or written off?
  • Is the mileage genuine, and can you show anything that backs it up?

Honest sellers answer these without getting annoyed. Vague answers, missing paperwork and pressure to decide today are all reasons to slow down.

How this protects you

The MOT history is hard to fake, because the record comes straight from the test centres. It gives you proof of mileage over time, a rough picture of how the car was maintained, and a list of known faults to check in person. It can also save you money: a car with a short MOT and a page of advisories is worth less than the asking price, and the record gives you fair grounds to negotiate. It is not a complete history — it will not show accidents, services or previous owners — so pair it with a proper look at the car, a test drive, and a paid history check if the car is expensive.

Quick answers

Is the MOT history check really free?

Yes. The service on GOV.UK is free, needs no account, and works for any car, motorcycle or van registered in the UK. You only need the number plate.

How far back does the MOT history go?

For cars, motorcycles and vans tested in England, Scotland or Wales, the computerised record goes back to 2005. Tests in Northern Ireland are shown from 2017.

Can I check the MOT history of a car I do not own?

Yes. The basic record is public, which is exactly why it is so useful when buying. You only need the V5C reference number if you want extra detail such as where each test took place.

What if the car has advisories on its latest test?

Advisories are warnings, not failures. Some are cheap, like a tyre nearing its limit. Others, like corrosion or play in the steering, can be costly. Price the repairs before you agree a deal.

Does a clean MOT history mean the car is a good buy?

Not on its own. It means the car passed its tests and the paperwork lines up. You still need to inspect the car, drive it, and check its service history and accident record separately.

Before you pay anything, run the free official check yourself on GOV.UK — it takes a minute and it is the seller's claims, written down by someone with no reason to bend the truth.
