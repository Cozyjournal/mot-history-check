Taxi and Private Hire MOT: First Test, Fees and Council Rules

Last reviewed: November 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

A taxi's MOT is not the same as a private car's. The test itself is the same class 4 check, at the same maximum fee of £54.85 — but a taxi needs its first test at one year old, not three, and the MOT is only half the story. The council or authority that licenses the vehicle can demand extra tests of its own, and in London that means two MOTs a year. Most guides cover either the MOT or the licence, and drivers get caught out in the gap between the two. This guide covers both, using GOV.UK's fees table and the Department for Transport's licensing guidance, so you know when the first test is due, what it costs, and what your council can ask for on top.

When does a taxi need its first MOT?

The three-year rule that most drivers know does not apply to taxis. GOV.UK's MOT fees table sets the age of the first test by vehicle type, and the split for hire vehicles is:

Taxis and ambulances: first MOT at 1 year. A taxi needs its first test one year after registration, then one every year after that. The class is class 4 and the maximum fee is £54.85.

Private hire and public service vehicles with up to 8 seats: first MOT at 3 years. A private hire car — the kind booked through an app or a minicab office — follows the normal three-year rule if it has eight passenger seats or fewer. It is also class 4, with the same £54.85 maximum fee.

Private passenger vehicles and ambulances with 9 to 12 seats: first MOT at 1 year. Bigger passenger vehicles are tested from one year old, and the maximum fee rises to £57.30. If the vehicle also needs a seat belt installation check, that is a class 4a test with a maximum fee of £64.

The difference is about how the vehicle earns its living. A taxi can be hailed on the street and works far harder than a private car, so it is tested sooner. A private hire car with up to eight seats is treated like an ordinary car for the first test — but that is only the MOT. The licence conditions in the next section often mean it is tested more often anyway. The MOT is only half the story: the licence

An MOT and a taxi licence are two different things, and you need both. The MOT is the national roadworthiness test, carried out at an approved MOT centre. The licence is issued by the local authority — the council outside London, Transport for London (TfL) inside it — and it is the licence that lets you carry paying passengers.

The licensing authority sets its own conditions, and those conditions can include extra vehicle tests. That is why two drivers in different towns can face different testing schedules for identical cars: the MOT law is the same everywhere, but the licence conditions are local. The Department for Transport's best practice guidance for councils says the legal minimum is an annual MOT for taxis of any age, and an annual test for private hire vehicles once they are three years old — and it recommends an annual test for licensed vehicles of whatever age, with twice-yearly tests for older vehicles.

So the practical rule is simple: the MOT tells you the legal minimum, and your licensing authority tells you the rest. Always read the current licence conditions for the council that licenses your car before you assume a yearly MOT is enough.

How often does a licensed car get tested?

Because the extra tests are licence conditions, not MOT law, there is no single national answer. The pattern across the country looks like this:

In London, two MOTs a year. Transport for London requires every licensed taxi and private hire car more than 12 months from its date of manufacture to pass two MOTs a year as a condition of its yearly licence: one issued no more than 14 days before the yearly licensing inspection, and a second six months after the licence starts. The reason TfL gives is plain — the six-monthly tests keep the car safe for passengers through the whole licence year.

Outside London, it depends on the council — and often on the car's age. Many councils tie the second test to age. Common patterns in published council conditions are a six-monthly test once the car passes five years old, or a council compliance inspection every six months for cars over five years, on top of the annual MOT. Some councils run their own testing centre for licensed vehicles. Others ask for an MOT plus a separate council inspection that checks the things an MOT does not — the meter, the roof sign, the livery and plates, and the interior condition.

An MOT certificate always runs for 12 months. The twice-yearly requirement does not change that: the second test produces a second certificate, and each one is valid for a year from its own test date. What changes is how often you have to prove the car is roadworthy to keep the licence.

If you are buying a car to use as a taxi or for private hire, check the licensing authority's current conditions before you buy. A car that is cheap because it is five years old can become expensive if your council wants it tested twice a year from that age.

What the taxi MOT checks

The MOT itself checks the same items as any class 4 test: brakes, steering, suspension, tyres, lights, visibility, seat belts, body and structure, exhaust and emissions. There is no separate "taxi MOT" with different items — the difference is the age of the first test and how often the licence demands one.

What the MOT does not check is the taxi equipment. The meter, the roof sign, the livery, the door signs and the licence plates are licensing matters, and they are checked by the council's own inspection, not by the MOT tester. A car can pass its MOT and still fail a council compliance check on a missing meter seal or a damaged roof sign — and a car can fail its MOT on brakes while its taxi equipment is perfect. Keep the two checks separate in your head, and budget time for both.

One thing that does catch hire cars out at the MOT is wear. A taxi or private hire car can cover two or three times the mileage of a private car in a year, so tyres, brakes and suspension wear faster, and advisories pile up sooner. Our pre-MOT checklist covers the 15 home checks worth doing before any test, and our guide to MOT advisories explained explains what to do when the tester flags wear that is not yet a fail.

What it costs, and keeping the renewal date

The maximum fee for a taxi MOT is £54.85 — the same as any class 4 car. A 9 to 12 seat passenger vehicle costs up to £57.30, and a class 4a seat belt installation check up to £64. Test centres can charge less than the maximum, but never more, and there is no VAT on the fee. Two MOTs a year means two fees, so a London driver pays up to £109.70 a year in test fees alone, before any council inspection charge.

The early-testing rule works the same as for any car: you can test up to a month (minus a day) before the due date and keep the same renewal date. For a car tested twice a year for its licence, that window matters — book the second test inside it and the dates stay tidy. Our guide to when your MOT is due explains the rule with worked examples.

Failing, retests and driving without an MOT

If the car fails, the retest rules are the same as for any vehicle. Leave the car at the test centre for repair and it can have a partial retest free if it is retested within 10 working days. Take it away and bring it back by the end of the next working day, and a partial retest on the failed items is also free. After that, a full test at the full fee is usually needed. Our guide to what to do if your car fails its MOT explains the retest rules in full.

Driving without a valid MOT is a separate risk for a hire driver. You cannot drive or park a vehicle on the road if the MOT has run out — the only exceptions are driving to a pre-booked test or to somewhere to be repaired — and the fine can be up to £1,000. On top of that, a licensing authority can suspend or refuse to renew a taxi or private hire licence if the vehicle is not properly tested. An expired MOT does not just stop the car; it can stop the work. Check the date on GOV.UK before the licence renewal comes round, not after.

Quick answers

When does a taxi need its first MOT?

A taxi needs its first MOT one year after registration, not three. GOV.UK's MOT fees table lists ambulances and taxis as needing their first test at one year old. A private hire or public service vehicle with up to eight seats follows the normal rule — first test at three years.

How much does a taxi MOT cost?

The maximum fee for a taxi MOT is £54.85, the same as a class 4 car test. A larger passenger vehicle with 9 to 12 seats costs up to £57.30, and a class 4a seat belt installation check costs up to £64. Test centres can charge less, but never more.

Do taxis need an MOT every 6 months?

Not by MOT law — an MOT certificate always runs for 12 months. But the council or authority that licenses the vehicle can require extra tests as a licence condition. In London, Transport for London requires licensed taxis and private hire cars over 12 months old to pass two MOTs a year. Other councils set their own rules, often tying twice-yearly tests to the car's age.

Is a council taxi inspection the same as an MOT?

No. The MOT is the national roadworthiness test, done at an approved MOT centre. A council compliance inspection is a separate licensing check, often covering the meter, roof sign, livery, plates and interior condition — things the MOT does not check. Many councils require both.

Can I drive my taxi if its MOT has expired?

No. You cannot drive or park a vehicle on the road without a valid MOT, except to a pre-booked test or to somewhere to be repaired. Driving without an MOT can mean a fine of up to £1,000, and as a licensed driver you also risk your taxi or private hire licence.

To sum up: a taxi needs its first MOT at one year old, not three — a private hire car with up to eight seats follows the normal three-year rule, and larger passenger vehicles are tested from one year. The test itself is the standard class 4 check, capped at £54.85, but the licence is a second layer: your council or TfL can require extra tests, and in London that means two MOTs a year. The MOT does not check taxi equipment like the meter or roof sign — that is the council's inspection. Keep both dates in mind, test early to hold the renewal date, and never let the MOT lapse while the car is working: the fine is up to £1,000, and the licence is at stake too.
