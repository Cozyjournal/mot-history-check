MOT History — Frequently Asked Questions

Written by MOT History Check editorial team Last updated: October 2024 15 Authoritative DVSA Answers

Checking a vehicle's MOT history on GOV.UK is completely free and requires only the vehicle registration number. Digital records date back to 2005 for England, Scotland, and Wales. Below are direct, fact-checked answers to the 15 most common MOT history, test validity, defect classification, and road law questions.

No. The official DVSA MOT history database requires a valid UK vehicle registration number (number plate) to retrieve test records. You cannot search by Vehicle Identification Number (VIN / Chassis Number), engine code, make/model, or owner name. If buying a used car and the seller has concealed the plate in photos, ask them directly for the VRM before viewing.

Digital records for vehicles tested in England, Scotland, and Wales date back to 2005, when the DVSA computerized test stations nationwide. Every annual test result, refusal reason, advisory note, and recorded mileage since 2005 is preserved online permanently.

Yes, 100% free. The official GOV.UK service provides vehicle safety and mileage history at zero cost. You do not need to register an account, download an app, or provide bank details. Beware of third-party websites asking for subscription fees or one-off payments to view standard MOT records.

An MOT test is a statutory visual and functional inspection enforced by the DVSA to ensure a vehicle meets minimum legal road safety and environmental limits. A tester does not dismantle parts or replace components. In contrast, a service is mechanical maintenance prescribed by the manufacturer, which includes replacing engine oil, filters, spark plugs, fluids, and wear items.

Every time a vehicle attends an MOT test, the technician records the exact odometer mileage into the DVSA terminal. By reviewing the year-over-year progression on GOV.UK, buyers can immediately see if a car's mileage suddenly decreased between tests, revealing illegal clocking or an unrecorded instrument cluster swap.

An advisory indicates that a part has begun to wear out (e.g. brake pads wearing thin or tyre near 2mm) but still complies with safety minimums; it issues an MOT PASS. An MOT failure occurs when a fault is classified as Major or Dangerous, making the vehicle non-compliant and requiring immediate repair.

You can replace a lost paper certificate online for free on GOV.UK. You simply need the vehicle registration number and the 11-digit reference number from your V5C registration certificate (logbook). You can immediately download and print a duplicate VT20 certificate PDF.

Yes. The GOV.UK MOT history database covers Class 1 and 2 (motorcycles), Class 4 (cars, taxis, and light commercial vans up to 3,000kg), and Class 7 (commercial vehicles 3,000kg to 3,500kg). Heavy Goods Vehicles (HGVs) and public service buses have annual test records managed under the commercial vehicle testing scheme.

Vehicle testing in Northern Ireland is administered separately by the Driver and Vehicle Agency (DVA). Northern Ireland records are checked online through the official nidirect.gov.uk vehicle testing system rather than the GB DVSA tool. However, if an NI car is subsequently registered or tested in Great Britain, its tests will appear on the DVSA system.

No. UK motor insurance underwriters do not calculate premiums based on past MOT failure records, provided the vehicle currently holds a valid pass certificate. However, driving a car with an expired MOT or with known dangerous defects can invalidate insurance claims in the event of an accident.

If the test records a Dangerous defect, you cannot drive the car under any circumstances; driving it risks a fine of up to £2,500 and 3 penalty points. If the defect is Major, you may only drive it away if your previous certificate is still valid and the fault does not render the vehicle unroadworthy under Road Traffic Act standards; otherwise, it may only be driven directly to a pre-booked garage for repairs.

A standard Class 4 MOT inspection takes between 45 and 60 minutes. Testing garages frequently request that owners leave the vehicle for half a day or a full working day to accommodate garage scheduling, re-tests, or minor bulb/wiper adjustments.

It is illegal to keep any vehicle on a public highway without a valid MOT, even if it remains stationary and parked. Automated police ANPR patrols and council enforcement officers can issue a penalty notice or order the vehicle clamped and removed to an impound facility. To park legally without an MOT, the vehicle must be kept off-road (on a private driveway or garage) under a Statutory Off Road Notification (SORN).
