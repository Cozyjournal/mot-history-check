What Does an MOT History Check Show?

Last reviewed: October 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

Short answer: a lot more than most people expect. The free GOV.UK service shows every MOT test a vehicle has taken since its records were computerised — whether it passed or failed, the mileage on the day, why it failed if it did, and the small faults the tester noticed along the way. This page goes through each part of the record, and just as importantly, what the record leaves out.

The current MOT status and expiry date

At the top of the record you see whether the vehicle has a valid MOT right now, and the date it runs out. This is the date that matters for taxing and driving the vehicle. If you are checking your own car, this is also where you confirm exactly when is my MOT due so you can book the next test in good time.

Pass or fail result for every test

Each test is listed with its date and its result. A pass means the vehicle met the minimum safety and environmental standards on that day. A fail means it did not, and the vehicle needed repairs and a retest. Older failures are not a disaster — most cars fail at some point in a long life — but the list lets you see how often it happened and whether the same faults kept coming back. Our guide on how to check MOT history shows you how to pull up this list in under a minute.

The mileage at each test

Next to every test result sits the odometer reading the tester recorded that day. Line the readings up in date order and you get the vehicle's mileage story, year by year. That story is one of the most useful parts of the whole record. Steady increases are what you want to see. A reading that drops below the previous year can mean the odometer was wound back, the instrument cluster was replaced, or the tester typed the number in wrong. When buying a used car, this is the first thing many people check.

Failure reasons in detail

For every failed test, the record lists the parts that failed and why. Faults are graded: dangerous defects mean the vehicle should not be driven at all until fixed, major defects cause a fail, and minor defects are recorded but the vehicle can still pass with them. The wording follows the tester's codes, so "Offside Front Suspension arm ball joint excessively worn" tells you exactly which corner of the car needed work. Seeing the same part fail repeatedly is a sign of cheap repairs or a car that has been neglected.

Advisory notices

Advisories are the in-between category: things the tester spotted that were not bad enough to fail the vehicle, but are on their way out. A tyre close to the legal tread limit, slight brake wear, or the start of corrosion are typical examples. They stay on the record, so you can see whether the owner dealt with them or left them to get worse. Our page on MOT advisories explained goes through the common ones and what they usually cost to put right.

Test centre information and certificates

The record holds an MOT test number for each test, and you can download copies of the current and previous MOT certificates for vehicles tested in England, Scotland or Wales. Where each test was done is also available, but to see the test centre location you need the 11-digit reference number from the vehicle's V5C log book, not just the number plate. That extra step protects owners while still letting a genuine buyer check the details with the seller's paperwork in hand. The history may also flag whether the vehicle has been recalled by the manufacturer for a safety reason.

How far back the record goes

For cars, motorcycles and vans, the computerised record starts in 2005. Tests before that were recorded on paper and do not appear. Vehicles tested in Northern Ireland show results from 2017 onwards. So a 15-year-old car can have a long, readable history, while a classic car from the 1990s will only show its tests from 2005 on — the record begins with computerisation, not with the car.

What the MOT history does not show

This matters just as much. The MOT record will not tell you:

  • Accident history. A car can be crashed and repaired and still have a clean MOT record. Insurance write-off information sits elsewhere.
  • Service history. Oil changes, cambelt swaps and stamps in the service book are not part of the MOT record.
  • Previous owners. The record shows tests, not keepers. The number of former keepers is on the V5C, not here.
  • The car's current condition. A pass means it met the standard on the test date. A lot can wear out in the months after.
  • Repairs between tests. You see what failed and what was advised, not the invoices for work done in between.

Think of the MOT history as the car's official test report card: honest, dated and hard to edit, but only covering the tests themselves.

Quick answers

Is the MOT history check free?

Yes. The GOV.UK service costs nothing, needs no account, and works with just the vehicle's number plate.

Can anyone see a vehicle's MOT history?

The main record is public, which is helpful when you are buying a vehicle. Test centre locations need the reference number from the V5C log book.

Does the MOT history show who owned the car?

No. It shows test results, mileages and defects. It does not list previous keepers, their names or their addresses.

Does it show if a car has been in an accident?

No. Accidents and insurance write-offs are not recorded in the MOT history. You need a separate vehicle history check for that.

Why does the record stop at 2005?

Because that is when MOT records were computerised for cars, motorcycles and vans in Great Britain. Paper-era results were never added to the online record.

The record only takes a minute to read, and it is free — run the official check on GOV.UK before you buy a vehicle, tax one, or book its next test.
