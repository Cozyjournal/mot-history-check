Modified Car MOT: What Passes, What Fails, What to Fix

Last reviewed: November 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

A modified car MOT is the same test as any other MOT, the tester is not marking you down for having a modified car. The car is checked against the same standards as a standard one: lights, brakes, tyres, steering, suspension, structure, exhaust and emissions. A modification only becomes a problem when it takes the car outside those standards. Most modified cars pass without trouble. The ones that fail usually fail on the same handful of changes: headlight conversions, dark front tints, decat exhausts, tyres that rub, and stretched tyres. This guide goes through each one, with the actual rule from the DVSA MOT inspection manual, so you can fix the problem before the test instead of paying for a retest after it.

The modified car MOT rule in one sentence

The tester does not care what the car looks like. They care whether it is safe, whether other road users can see it and be seen by it, and whether it meets the emissions limits for its age. A well-fitted modification that respects those three things passes. A cheap or careless one that does not, fails, and the fail is recorded against the same defect categories as any other car. Our guide to common MOT failure reasons explains how those categories work.

Headlights and lighting: the biggest trap

Aftermarket lighting is the single most common reason a modified car fails, and the rule is stricter than most owners realise.

HID and LED conversions in halogen headlamps. On cars first used on or after 1 April 1986, existing halogen headlamp units must not be converted to be used with high intensity discharge (HID) or LED bulbs. If such a conversion has been done, the tester must fail the headlamp for "light source and lamp not compatible". It is a major fault, and there is no discretion, the beam pattern from a bulb the housing was never designed for scatters light everywhere and blinds other drivers. The one exception is an approved LED replacement bulb (LEDr) that carries a manufacturer-issued approval sticker permanently fixed to the vehicle. If your car left the factory with LED headlights, the whole unit was designed around them and there is no problem at all, the rule targets conversions, not original equipment. The full wording is in section 4.1.4 of the DVSA MOT inspection manual: lamps, reflectors and electrical equipment.

Beam aim. Even a legal headlamp fails if it cannot be aimed correctly. Lowered cars often sit with the beam pointing too low or too high, and if the aim cannot be brought within limits the car fails. Get the aim checked after any suspension change.

Smoked and tinted lamp lenses. Tinted rear clusters and smoked headlight covers are legal only if the lights still meet the minimum output and the right colour: white or yellow at the front, red at the rear. Cheap aftermarket units often do not, and faded or stone-chipped covers can reduce output enough to fail on their own. Clean the lenses before the test.

Extra lighting. Underbody neon, angel eyes and halo rings that are lit while the engine is running can fail if they are visible from outside and confuse the car's lighting. Disconnect anything decorative before the test.

Tinted windows: the percentages

The tint rules are fixed numbers, and they apply to the front of the car only:

Front windscreen: at least 75% of light must get through.

Front side windows: at least 70% of light must get through.

Rear side windows and rear windscreen: no MOT limit.

The tester checks visibility through the front windows rather than measuring the tint percentage directly, but a front tint darker than the limit restricts the driver's view of the road and fails. Rear tints, however dark, are not an MOT matter. If you are unsure about a front tint, peel it off before the test, it is cheaper than a fail and a retest.

Suspension, wheels and tyres

Lowering a car is not a fail in itself. What fails is what the lowering does to the rest of the car.

Lowered suspension. Coilovers and lowering springs pass if the tyres do not rub on the arches, inner wings or suspension parts at full steering lock and full suspension travel, if the headlights can still be aimed correctly, and if the dampers still have enough travel to work. A car dropped so low that the dampers have almost no travel left, or that scrapes over every speed bump, is very likely to fail on clearance or geometry. Check for contact with the steering at full lock in both directions before you book.

Aftermarket alloys and wheel spacers. Wheels pass if they are correctly seated, the tyres are rated for the car's load and speed, and nothing rubs. Spacers must be secured with wheel studs of the correct length and properly torqued, loose or short stud engagement fails.

Stretched tyres. A stretched tyre fails if the bead has visibly come away from the rim, or if the tyre's load or speed rating is too low for the car. The stance look is fine; a tyre that is not properly seated on its wheel is not.

Exhaust and emissions

Exhaust noise. The manual says exhaust noise must not be unreasonably above what you would expect from the same car with a standard silencer. A loud aftermarket system is judged against that standard, and an excessive one fails. A cat-back system that is not obviously louder than the original and is securely mounted will normally pass.

Decat pipes and removed emission equipment. On cars first used from 1 September 2002, emission control equipment fitted by the manufacturer that is missing or obviously modified is a major fault. That includes a removed catalytic converter, a deleted EGR valve and a disabled AdBlue system, and a decat car will also fail the emissions test itself. On a diesel, evidence that the particulate filter (DPF) has been tampered with is a fail too. Our diesel car MOT guide explains the smoke limits and the DPF rules in detail.

Remaps and bigger turbos. A remap is not checked directly, but the car still has to meet its emission limits, and a remap that pushes smoke or gases over the limit fails the same way any other emissions failure does.

Body, structure, seats and number plates

Structural changes. Cuts to chassis members, welded-in roll cages near the prescribed corrosion areas, and any modification to crash structure can fail the MOT outright. If you have cut metal to fit something, get it checked by a professional before the test.

Seats and seatbelts. Removing seats is fine only if the seatbelt mountings that went with them are not left damaged or insecure, and every seatbelt fitted must work. Aftermarket seats and harnesses must be securely mounted, and the belt must latch and release properly.

Number plates. A modified or styled number plate fails if the characters are not in the legal font and spacing, or if the plate is cracked, faded or unreadable. Show plates and illegal spacing are an easy fail to avoid, put the legal plates back on for the test.

What to do before the test

Work through this list the week before your MOT, and most modified-car fails disappear:

Refit the standard bulbs if you have LED or HID conversions in halogen headlamps, or fit complete headlamp units designed for LED or HID.

Check the beam aim after any suspension change, and check for tyre rubbing at full lock in both directions.

Peel off dark front tints and clean smoked lamp lenses.

Refit the catalytic converter and DPF if either has been removed or gutted.

Disconnect decorative lighting and refit the legal number plates.

Check the dashboard. A warning light showing a fault, engine management, ABS or airbag, is a fail on its own. Our guide to dashboard warning lights that fail an MOT lists every light that matters.

If the car was imported already modified, the same rules apply at its first UK test, our guide to MOT for imported cars covers the IVA test, the first MOT and the UK conversions to sort out first.

Quick answers

Will a modified car pass an MOT?

Yes, if the modifications are fitted properly and the car still meets the same safety, lighting, emissions and visibility standards as a standard car. A modification only fails when it takes the car outside those standards, for example a headlight conversion that ruins the beam pattern, or a removed catalytic converter.

Do LED bulbs in halogen headlights fail an MOT?

Yes, on cars first used on or after 1 April 1986. The DVSA MOT inspection manual says existing halogen headlamp units must not be converted to HID or LED bulbs, and a converted unit is failed for "light source and lamp not compatible". The only exception is an approved LED replacement bulb with a manufacturer-issued approval sticker on the vehicle.

Will a lowered car pass an MOT?

Usually yes. Lowering itself is not a fail. The car fails if the tyres rub on the arches or suspension parts, if the headlights cannot be aimed correctly, or if the suspension has so little travel left that it cannot do its job. Check for rubbing at full steering lock before the test.

Does a decat exhaust fail an MOT?

Yes. On cars first used from 1 September 2002, emission control equipment fitted by the manufacturer that is missing or obviously modified is a major fault. A removed catalytic converter also means the car will fail the emissions test, and on a diesel, evidence that the particulate filter has been tampered with is a fail too.

How dark can window tints be for an MOT?

The front windscreen must let through at least 75% of light, and the front side windows at least 70%. There is no MOT limit on the rear side windows or the rear windscreen. A front tint darker than the limit fails because it restricts the driver's view of the road.

To sum up: a modified car MOT is the same test as any other, judged against the same standards. The changes that fail are the ones that make the car less safe or less visible, headlight conversions in halogen units, front tints darker than 75% and 70%, decat exhausts and tampered DPFs, tyres that rub, and stretched tyres that are not seated. Fix those before the test, and the rest of the car is judged exactly like a standard one.
