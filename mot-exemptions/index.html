MOT exemptions: the 40-year historic rule and legal requirements

Last reviewed: October 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

Most vehicles first registered more than 40 years ago that have not undergone substantial modifications are legally exempt from annual MOT testing in the UK. However, being exempt from the annual test does not exempt you from the law: vehicle owners remain under a strict legal duty to keep their vehicle in a roadworthy condition at all times.

The rules governing vehicle exemptions balance preserving British automotive heritage with maintaining public road safety standards. Below is a detailed breakdown of how the rolling 40-year exemption functions, what constitutes a "substantial change", how to declare exemption, and other specialised categories exempt from testing.

How the rolling 40-year historic vehicle rule works

Under regulations introduced by the Department for Transport in May 2018, vehicles of historical interest benefit from a rolling exemption scheme based on the date of their construction or initial registration.

The exemption applies automatically on a rolling calendar basis:

  • A vehicle becomes eligible for MOT exemption on the exact date it reaches 40 years of age from its date of manufacture.
  • If the exact build date is not recorded on your V5C registration document, the 40-year period is calculated from the date the vehicle was first registered with the DVLA.
  • For example, in 2026, vehicles manufactured or registered in 1986 or earlier qualify for exemption, provided they satisfy the "substantial change" criteria. In 2027, vehicles manufactured up to 1987 will qualify, and so forth each successive year.

What qualifies as a "Substantial Change"?

To qualify for the 40-year exemption as a "Vehicle of Historic Interest" (VHI), the machine must remain fundamentally true to its original design. If critical structural, steering, suspension, or powertrain assemblies have been substantially altered over the previous 30 years, the vehicle forfeits its MOT exemption and must undergo annual testing.

According to official DVSA guidance, a vehicle has undergone a substantial change if it meets any of the following conditions:

  1. Chassis or monocoque bodyshell alteration: The chassis or main load-bearing structure has been modified, replaced with a non-original specification, or fundamentally altered (excluding sympathetic repairs using original-specification replacement panels).
  2. Suspension or steering overhaul: The primary design architecture of the suspension or steering assembly has been replaced (such as replacing a leaf-spring axle with a modern independent coil-over setup, or replacing a steering box with modern rack-and-pinion power steering).
  3. Engine substitution of a different type: The engine has been replaced with a power unit that differs in number of cylinders, displacement, or fuel type from the manufacturer's original specification, significantly altering performance.

Importantly, changes made to improve safety, efficiency, or environmental emissions (such as fitting seatbelts, converting to electronic ignition, or fitting disc brakes where drums were standard) do not count as substantial changes and will not invalidate your historic exemption.

The legal obligation to maintain roadworthiness

One of the most dangerous misconceptions among classic car owners is believing that MOT exemption means the vehicle is immune from inspection or policing. This is entirely incorrect.

Under the Road Traffic Act 1988 (Section 40A), it is a serious criminal offence to use, cause, or permit another person to use a motor vehicle on a road if its condition poses a danger of injury to any person. Even without an annual MOT certificate:

  • Tyres must always have at least 1.6mm of tread depth and be free of structural bulges or cord exposure.
  • All lights, direction indicators, and horn mechanisms must work correctly whenever driven.
  • Braking efficiency, steering linkages, and suspension mounts must remain fully serviceable.
  • Chassis and body structures must not suffer from hazardous rust or sharp, jagged edges.

Police officers and DVSA roadside enforcement examiners have statutory powers to stop any vehicle on public roads, conduct immediate roadworthiness spot-checks, and issue prohibition notices (form PG9) or court summonses if dangerous defects are found. Convictions carry fines of up to £2,500 and 3 penalty points per safety-critical defect.

How to declare your vehicle exempt (Form V112)

While qualifying historic vehicles do not need to book an annual MOT test, the exemption must be formally declared to the DVLA when taxing your vehicle each year. Even though historic vehicles also qualify for free vehicle excise duty (VED), you must still "tax" the vehicle annually via the DVLA online portal or at a Post Office branch.

When applying for road tax for an exempt vehicle, you complete a digital or paper declaration known as Form V112 (Declaration of exemption from MOT). By submitting this form, you legally certify that your vehicle meets all historical criteria and has not undergone substantial modifications.

Other vehicles exempt from standard Class 4 MOT tests

Beyond 40-year-old historic vehicles, several other vehicle types are exempt from standard Class 4 testing:

  • Electric goods vehicles: Small electric commercial goods vehicles registered before 1 March 2015.
  • Tractors and agricultural machines: Agricultural tractors, harvesters, and works trucks driven exclusively for farming operations or within a limited statutory mileage radius between holdings.
  • Track-laying and specialised machinery: Steam-powered vehicles, military equipment, and road-laying machinery.
  • Vehicles driven solely on offshore islands: Vehicles maintained on designated UK offshore islands where there is no road connection to the mainland and no authorised MOT testing facility.
