MOT for Imported Cars: IVA, the First Test and What to Change First

Last reviewed: November 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

An imported car needs an MOT just like any other car — the test itself is the same class 4 test, at the same maximum fee of £54.85, checking the same brakes, lights, tyres, suspension and emissions. What is different is everything that happens before the test: the car has to be approved for UK roads, registered with the DVLA, and converted for driving on the left. Most guides skip that part and jump straight to the test, which is why imported cars fail on headlights, speedometers and fog lights that could have been sorted weeks earlier. This guide follows the GOV.UK import rules step by step, so you know what to do first, when the first MOT is due, and what the tester will be looking at.

The MOT itself is the same test

Once an imported car is registered in the UK, there is nothing special about its MOT. It is a class 4 test — the same as for a car bought in a UK showroom — and it takes the usual 45 to 60 minutes. Our guide to how long an MOT takes explains what happens during that hour. The tester checks the same items, the fee is capped at the same £54.85, and the result is recorded on the same DVSA system. Our MOT cost guide lists the maximum fee for every vehicle class.

The differences are all before the test. A car built for another market was not approved for sale in the UK, so it has to be shown to meet UK safety and environmental standards before it can be registered — and it cannot be taxed or driven on UK roads until that is done. Get the approval and registration steps right first, and the MOT itself is straightforward.

The order: approval first, MOT second

GOV.UK sets out six steps for bringing a vehicle permanently into Great Britain, and the order matters. You can be prosecuted if you use the vehicle on a public road before you complete them, unless you are driving it to a pre-booked MOT or vehicle approval test:

1. Make an import declaration. You usually need to pay a shipping company or customs agent to do this for you, either before the car arrives or at the UK border.

2. Pay VAT and customs duty at the UK border. Your shipping company or customs agent will usually arrange this. You may be able to claim relief — for example, if you are moving to the UK permanently or bringing back a car you previously owned here.

3. Tell HMRC within 14 days that the vehicle has arrived, by making a Notification of Vehicle Arrivals (NOVA) declaration. You cannot register the vehicle until this is done, and you may be fined if you are late.

4. Get vehicle approval to show the car meets safety and environmental standards. This is the step that decides whether the car needs an IVA test, a certificate of conformity, or nothing at all — see the next section.

5. Register and tax the vehicle with the DVLA. They will give you a registration number so you can get number plates made up. The registration fee is £55, and it can take up to 6 weeks for the V5C logbook to arrive.

6. Insure the vehicle before you drive it on UK roads.

The MOT sits inside this sequence, not after it: an imported car that is over ten years old usually takes its MOT as part of the approval step, before registration. A newer car takes an IVA test instead, and its first MOT comes later, once it is registered.

Vehicle approval: IVA or MOT, by the car's age

Whether the car needs an IVA test or an MOT before registration depends on its age and where it was registered before:

Under ten years old, not registered in the EU. The car needs Individual Vehicle Approval (IVA). IVA checks a car built for another market against UK standards, and it is more thorough than an MOT, because the car was never approved for sale here. Motorcycles and smaller vehicles take Motorcycle Single Vehicle Approval (MSVA) instead.

Under ten years old, registered in the EU. Get a European Certificate of Conformity from the manufacturer to show the car has approval. If it is a left-hand drive car, you will also need a certificate of GB conversion IVA, which costs £100 — or an IVA test instead, for example if the car is a lorry or goods vehicle over 3,500kg.

Over ten years old. The car may not need vehicle approval at all. Instead, it takes an MOT — after the UK conversions it needs, usually headlights, a speedometer and a rear fog light. This is the route most Japanese imports over ten years old take, and it is also the route for cars between 30 and 40 years old, which pay no import duty and 5% import VAT instead of 20% if they are unaltered.

Over 40 years old. The car does not need a test before registration, although you can choose to have an MOT anyway. Cars over 40 years old that have not been substantially changed in the last 30 years are also exempt from the annual MOT once registered — our MOT exemptions guide explains the historic vehicle rules in full.

Motor caravans and ambulances are exempt from type approval and single vehicle approval. Their only pre-registration test is the MOT: a new imported motor caravan takes its first MOT three years after first UK registration, and an imported ambulance one year after first UK registration. Our motorhome MOT guide covers the class rules for motor caravans.

When is the first MOT due on an import?

For a car that has been registered in the UK, the rule is the same as for any car: the first MOT is due by the third anniversary of its first UK registration, and then one every year. In Northern Ireland, cars are tested from four years old. Our guide to the first MOT on a new car explains the three-year rule and the early-testing rule — you can test up to a month (minus a day) before the due date and keep the same renewal date.

There is one difference for imports. If the car was used on the road before it was registered in the UK, the first MOT is due three years from the end of the year it was manufactured. So a car made at any time in 2014 and used abroad before import needs its first MOT no later than 31 December 2017. The date that counts is the date on the V5C logbook once the car is registered — check Section 4 of the V5C for the date of first registration, and check the date free on the official GOV.UK MOT history service if you are not sure. Our guide to when your MOT is due explains how to check and what the early-testing rule means for the renewal date.

The UK conversions to make before the test

A car built for another market usually needs a few changes before it can pass a UK test. These are the conversions that come up most often, and they are worth doing before the test rather than after a fail:

Headlights. This is the single most common fail on imported cars. Headlights built for driving on the right dip the beam the wrong way, and they will dazzle oncoming drivers on UK roads. The headlamp aim and pattern are checked at the MOT, and a wrong pattern is a fail. Most imports need replacement headlight units or proper beam deflectors — not just a sticker — and Japanese cars often need the whole unit changed.

Speedometer. The speedometer must read in miles per hour. A speedo that only shows kilometres per hour fails the test. A replacement dial face or a converted unit is usually needed; a small sticker is not enough for most testers.

Rear fog light. UK cars must have a rear fog light, and many imports — especially Japanese domestic market cars — do not have one. Fitting a rear fog lamp, wired to a switch the driver can reach, is a standard part of an import conversion.

Front window tint. Heavy tint on the front windows usually has to come off. The windscreen and front side windows must let enough light through, and dark aftermarket tint is a common reason for a fail on imports.

Number plates. Once the DVLA has issued a registration number, the car needs UK-format number plates made up. Plates with the wrong font, spacing or size fail the test.

Do these conversions before the test, not after. A fail on headlights or a speedometer means a retest, and a retest after 10 working days — or at a different test centre — means a full test again at the full fee. Our guide to what to do if your car fails its MOT explains the retest rules.

What fails imported cars most at the MOT

Once the conversions are done, an imported car is tested like any other car — but a few items catch imports out more often than UK cars:

Headlamp aim and pattern. Even after conversion, the aim has to be right. A headlight that is set too high, too low or still dipping the wrong way is a fail. This is the item to check first if you are presenting an import.

Emissions. Japanese imports in particular can fail the emissions test, often because the catalytic converter is missing, damaged or not the type the car was built with. Emission control equipment fitted by the manufacturer that is missing or obviously modified is a major fault. A diesel import gets the smoke test and the DPF checks — our diesel car MOT guide explains the smoke limits and the DPF rules, and our modified car MOT guide covers what else on a modified import can fail.

Tyres and brakes. Cars that have been shipped, stored or left standing can arrive with flat-spotted or perished tyres and corroded brakes. Check the tyre age and condition, and take the car for a proper run before the test.

Rust and underseal. Japanese imports are often clean underneath, but cars from other markets can arrive with corrosion that a UK tester will fail. Underseal can hide corrosion, and the tester will fail a car where the structure is weakened, even if it looks tidy from above.

VIN and identity. The tester checks the vehicle identification number against the records. A VIN that does not match the V5C, or that has been altered, is a problem — and for a damaged or salvaged import, the DVLA may give the car a Q registration number, a new VIN, or a marker on the V5C. A car classed as seriously damaged cannot be registered in the UK at all.

Grey imports: Japanese cars

Most imported cars in the UK are Japanese domestic market cars — "grey imports" that were never sold here officially. They follow the same rules as any import, but a few things are worth knowing:

Japanese cars over ten years old take an MOT instead of an IVA test, after the usual conversions: headlights, speedometer and a rear fog light. Under ten years old, they take an IVA test. The conversions are usually done by the importer before the car is offered for sale, but check before you buy — a car advertised as "MOT'd" should have its certificate, and you can check its history free on GOV.UK.

One trap when buying a grey import: the MOT history on GOV.UK only shows tests done in the UK. A car with no UK MOT history is not necessarily a problem — it may simply have been registered recently — but if the seller claims a long UK history, check it. Our guide to how to check MOT history shows how, using just the registration number, and our guide to checking MOT history when buying a used car explains what to look for in the results.

Visiting the UK? No MOT needed for up to 6 months

The rules above are for cars being imported permanently. If you are visiting the UK and do not usually live here, you can usually use a vehicle with foreign number plates without registering or taxing it, as long as it is registered and taxed in its home country and you only use it for up to 6 months in total — a single visit, or several shorter visits over 12 months.

You do not pay VAT or customs duty on a temporary import if it is for your own private use, you are not a UK resident, you do not sell, lend or hire it here, and you re-export it within 6 months. Claim the relief by filling in form C110 and taking the car through the "nothing to declare" channel when you arrive. If you become a resident or stay longer than 6 months, you must register and tax the vehicle — follow the permanent import steps above. And if your number plates show characters that are not identifiable in the UK, for example Arabic script, or the car is not registered in its home country, you will need temporary Q number plates from the DVLA first.

Bringing a UK car back

If you are bringing back a car that was registered in the UK before and then exported, the rules are simpler. You must tell HMRC within 14 days that the car has arrived, pay any VAT and customs duty you owe, and register and tax it with the DVLA — but you do not have to get vehicle approval, and you do not pay the £55 registration fee. Once the car has passed its MOT, register and tax it, and send the original foreign registration certificate with the application. If you do not want to tax the car yet — for example, because you plan to sell it or it is not roadworthy — you still need to register it, and you will need to make a SORN. Our SORN car MOT guide explains the rules for cars kept off the road.

Checking the MOT history of an import

Once an imported car has a UK registration number, its MOT history works like any other car's. Check it free on the official GOV.UK MOT history service using just the registration number — you will see every UK test, the result, the defects recorded, and the mileage at each test. To see where each test was done, GOV.UK asks for the 11-digit reference number from the V5C.

What you will not see is anything from before the car arrived in the UK. Tests done abroad are not on the DVSA system, so a recently imported car will show little or no history. That is normal. What matters is the history from the UK registration date onwards — and if a seller claims a car has been in the UK for years but GOV.UK shows only one test, that is worth asking about.

Quick answers

Does an imported car need an MOT in the UK?

Yes. Once an imported car is registered in the UK it needs an MOT on the same terms as any other car — a first test by the third anniversary of its first UK registration, then one every year. Before it can be registered, it may also need vehicle approval, such as an IVA test.

When is the first MOT due on an imported car?

Count three years from the date of its first UK registration. If the car was used on the road before it was registered here, the first MOT is due three years from the end of the year it was manufactured.

What is the difference between an IVA test and an MOT?

An IVA (Individual Vehicle Approval) test checks that a car built for another market meets UK safety and environmental standards, so it can be registered here. It is a one-off approval before registration. An MOT is the annual roadworthiness test that every registered car over three years old needs, every year.

What changes does an imported car need before its MOT?

The usual conversions are headlights with the correct UK dipped-beam pattern, a speedometer that reads in miles per hour, and a rear fog light. Heavy tint on the front windows usually has to come off too. These are the items that fail imported cars most often.

Can I drive an imported car to its MOT before it is registered?

You can be prosecuted if you use an imported vehicle on a public road before you complete the import steps, unless you are driving it to a pre-booked MOT or vehicle approval test. The car must also be insured before you drive it on UK roads.

To sum up: an imported car takes the same class 4 MOT as any other car, at a maximum of £54.85, but the work starts before the test. Tell HMRC within 14 days of arrival, get vehicle approval — an IVA test for a car under ten years old, or an MOT for an older car — then register and tax it with the DVLA for a £55 fee. The first MOT is due three years after first UK registration, or three years from the end of the year of manufacture if the car was used abroad before registration. Before the test, convert the headlights, the speedometer and the rear fog light, and remove heavy front tint — those are the items that fail imports most. And check the car's UK MOT history free on GOV.UK before you buy or book.
