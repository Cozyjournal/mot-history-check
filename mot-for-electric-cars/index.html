MOT for Electric & Hybrid Cars: What Is Checked (and What Is Not)

Last reviewed: October 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

Yes, electric cars need an MOT too. From three years old, an EV goes through the same yearly roadworthiness test as any petrol or diesel car. The big difference is what happens under the bonnet — or rather, what doesn't. There is no exhaust, so there is no emissions test. Here's how an electric or hybrid MOT works, what the tester looks at, and what catches owners out.

When does an electric or hybrid car need its first MOT?

The rules are the same as for any car. The first MOT is due when the car is three years old (four years in Northern Ireland), and then every year after that. Hybrids follow exactly the same timetable. There is no discount, delay or exemption for having a battery instead of a fuel tank, and driving without a valid MOT carries the same penalties as it does for any other car.

What is NOT checked

Exhaust emissions. A fully electric car has no tailpipe and produces no exhaust gases, so the emissions test simply doesn't apply. Less well known: hybrids skip the measured emissions test too. The official DVSA inspection manual lists hybrid vehicles — cars with both electric and combustion engines — among those that do not need the gaseous emissions check, because the tester cannot force the petrol or diesel engine to run on demand. On a hybrid, the tester still inspects the exhaust itself for security, leaks and noise, and the car must not pour out smoke.

Fuel system and engine oil. No fuel tank, lines or filter to inspect on a pure EV, and no engine oil level check either.

Battery health. This one surprises people. The MOT does not measure how much capacity your battery has lost, how far the car will go on a charge, or the state of the cells. A car with a tired battery and a much shorter range than new can still pass with a clean sheet.

What IS checked

Most of the test is identical to any other car:

  • Tyres. This is the big one for EVs. Electric cars are heavier than petrol equivalents and deliver their power instantly, so tyres tend to wear faster. The legal minimum is the same 1.6mm of tread, and worn or damaged tyres are among the most common MOT failure reasons for every type of car.
  • Brakes. The brakes are tested on rollers like any car. Regenerative braking means pads and discs often last longer, but discs that sit unused can corrode — and the tester still checks the whole system, including the handbrake and brake pipes.
  • Suspension and steering. The weight of the battery pack works the suspension hard, so worn joints and tired dampers are checked just as they are on any car.
  • Lights, wipers, windscreen, horn, mirrors and seatbelts. All the usual safety items, unchanged.
  • Bodywork and structure. Sharp edges, corrosion in load-bearing areas, and the general condition of the underbody — which on most EVs includes the battery casing underneath the car.

The battery and high-voltage checks

The DVSA manual tells testers they must inspect the batteries on all vehicles, including electric and hybrid ones. For the main traction battery it is a visual check only, and only where the battery can actually be seen — testers do not remove covers or strip anything down. They are looking for obvious problems: a damaged or insecure battery casing, signs of leaking, or damage to the high-voltage cabling, which is usually bright orange so it can be identified at a glance.

If a propulsion battery is leaking, the manual says the tester should refuse to carry out the test at all. Realistically, though, on most modern EVs the battery is sealed into the floor of the car and largely out of sight, so this part of the test is a look at what can be seen rather than a deep inspection. Anything flagged will appear on your certificate — our guide to MOT advisories explained shows how to tell the items that matter from the ones that don't.

How much does an EV MOT cost?

The same as any car. The maximum fee a test centre can charge is set by the government at £54.85 for a car, and that cap applies whatever powers it. Many garages charge less than the cap, so it is worth comparing prices — our MOT cost page explains the fees in full. Don't expect a discount because there's no emissions test: the fee covers the whole inspection, and the safety checks take just as long on an EV.

Why this matters more each year

Every year, a bigger wave of electric cars reaches its third birthday and its first MOT, and hybrids have been on the road in large numbers for well over a decade. Test centres now see battery-powered cars every day. For owners, the practical lesson is simple: the parts that fail EVs are mostly the same parts that fail everything else — tyres, brakes, suspension and lights — so the same basic checks before the test pay off.

Quick answers

Do hybrid cars get an emissions test?

Not the measured one. The DVSA inspection manual exempts hybrid vehicles from the gaseous emissions check, because the engine can't be kept running on demand. The exhaust is still checked for leaks, damage and noise.

Is the battery tested for health or range?

No. The tester looks at the battery's visible condition and security only. Capacity, range and cell health are not part of the MOT.

Do I need a special test centre for an electric car?

No. Any approved MOT test centre can test an electric or hybrid car — the class of test is the same as for a petrol or diesel car of the same size.

Can an electric car fail its MOT?

Absolutely. Tyres, brakes, suspension, lights and structural problems fail EVs just as they fail other cars. Electric power only removes the emissions part of the test.

Does an EV MOT cost less because it's quicker?

No — the official maximum fee is the same £54.85 cap, and most of the test (brakes, tyres, suspension, lights) takes the same time as any other car.

Whatever you drive, you can check any vehicle's MOT status and history free on the official GOV.UK service before you book your test.
