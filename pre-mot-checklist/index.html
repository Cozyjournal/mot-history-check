Pre-MOT Checklist: 15 Simple Checks to Do at Home Before Your Test

Last reviewed: October 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

A large share of MOT failures come from small things — a dead bulb, worn wiper blades, tyres past their best. None of these need a mechanic to spot. Work through this pre-MOT checklist in your driveway the week before your test and you can fix the cheap faults yourself instead of paying for a fail and a retest. The whole list takes about half an hour.

The 15 checks

1. Lights and indicators Switch everything on — headlights, full beam, indicators, hazards, brake lights and the number plate light — and walk around the car. Ask someone to press the brake pedal while you watch the rear. A single blown bulb is one of the most common MOT failure reasons, and most bulbs cost a few pounds to replace.

2. Tyres: tread and pressure The legal minimum tread depth is 1.6mm across the middle of the tyre. Use the 20p test: push a 20p coin into the grooves — if the outer band of the coin is hidden, the tread is fine; if you can see it, the tyre is near or below the limit. Check the pressures against the figures in your handbook or door sticker, and look for cuts, bulges or nails in the sidewalls.

3. Wipers and washers Top up the screenwash bottle. The blades should clear the windscreen cleanly without smearing, juddering or leaving big unwiped patches. Split or torn blades are cheap to swap and a straight fail if the tester can't see through the screen properly.

4. Horn Give it a short press. It has to work and be loud enough for other road users to hear. A silent horn is a fail.

5. Mirrors All mirrors the car was built with must be fitted, secure and give you a clear view behind. Cracked glass that distorts your view, or a mirror hanging loose, can fail.

6. Seatbelts Pull every belt out to its full length and check for cuts, fraying or damage. Click each one into its buckle and make sure it locks, holds firm with a tug, and releases properly. Belts that don't retract or buckles that don't click are failures.

7. Brake pedal feel With the engine on, press the brake pedal. It should feel firm and stop well before the floor. A pedal that feels spongy, sinks slowly, or needs pumping points to a problem that needs a garage, not a test.

8. Handbrake Park on a slope, apply the handbrake and take your foot off the brake pedal — the car should hold still. The lever should not pull up almost vertically or feel like it has run out of travel.

9. Exhaust smoke and noise Start the car and listen. The exhaust shouldn't rattle, blow loudly or sound much louder than usual. Watch for heavy smoke as the engine starts and revs — thick blue, black or white smoke is a bad sign for the emissions test.

10. Fluid leaks Look at the ground where the car is usually parked. Fresh patches of oil, coolant or brake fluid mean something is leaking. A damp patch is worth checking; a puddle means book a repair before the test, not after.

11. Number plates Both plates should be clean, firmly fixed and easy to read from a distance. Cracked, faded or loose plates can fail, and so can letters that are incorrectly spaced.

12. Windscreen Check for chips and cracks, especially in the area directly in front of the driver, where even a small chip can fail the test. Stone chips often spread, so a cheap chip repair now can save you a windscreen — and a fail — later.

13. Warning lights on the dash Turn the ignition on and watch the dashboard. Engine management, ABS and airbag warning lights that stay on once the engine is running can all mean a fail. Don't book the test hoping they'll be ignored — get the fault read and fixed first.

14. Fuel and oil levels Make sure there's enough fuel in the tank for the emissions test to be carried out (or enough charge in an electric car to complete the test), and check the oil level sits between the marks on the dipstick. Very low oil can stop the emissions check being done safely.

15. Driver's view of the road Clear anything that blocks your view: phone holders stuck in the wrong place, stickers, and items hanging from the rear-view mirror. Also give the windscreen a proper clean inside and out so the tester sees the same clear view you do.

How long does an MOT take?

The test itself usually takes around 45 minutes to an hour. Many garages ask you to drop the car off in the morning and collect it later, so plan for the car being away for a few hours even though the test is quicker. If the car fails and you agree to repairs on the spot, that adds time on top.

If the car still fails

Even good preparation can't catch everything. If your car fails, you usually have two cheap options: leave it at the test centre for repair and it can be retested free within 10 working days, or take it away and bring it back — for certain failed items, a partial retest by the end of the next working day is free. Quick answers

How much does an MOT cost?

The maximum a test centre can charge for a car is £54.85, and many garages charge less. Repairs and retests after a fail are extra, which is why the checks above are worth doing.

When should I do these checks?

About a week before the test. That leaves time to order bulbs, wiper blades or tyres and get anything bigger looked at, instead of finding out on the day.

Can a car fail for being dirty?

Dirt alone won't fail your car, but a cabin or boot packed with clutter can be a problem — the tester needs to reach the seatbelts and see everything properly, and a car that's extremely dirty inside can be turned away. A quick tidy helps.

My tyre tread is close to the limit. Will it pass?

If it measures at or above 1.6mm across the centre three-quarters of the tyre, it should pass — but expect an advisory, and plan to replace it soon. Below the limit anywhere on the tyre is a fail. See the common MOT failure reasons for more detail.

A warning light comes on only sometimes. Should I still book the test?

Get it checked first. If the light is on while the tester is working, it can fail the car, and an intermittent fault usually gets worse, not better.
