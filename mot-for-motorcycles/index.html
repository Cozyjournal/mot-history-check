Motorcycle MOT: What Gets Checked, What It Costs and the Classes Explained

Last reviewed: November 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

A motorcycle MOT is not a car MOT with fewer wheels. It has its own classes, its own fee, its own checklist — and no emissions test at all. Most guides online copy the car rules across and get the details wrong, from the tyre tread depth to the exhaust. This guide follows the official GOV.UK checklist and the DVSA motorcycle inspection manual, so every figure below is the one the tester actually uses.

When does a motorcycle need its first MOT?

By the third anniversary of its registration, and then every year after that. The rule is the same whether you ride a 50cc moped, a 125, or a 1,200cc tourer. An MOT lasts for a year, and you can get it done up to a month (minus a day) before the current one runs out and keep the same renewal date for the following year. If you are not sure of the date, check it free on the official GOV.UK MOT history service with just the registration number — our guide to when your MOT is due explains the dates and the early-testing rule in full.

Riding without a valid MOT is not a minor matter: you can be fined up to £1,000, and you cannot tax the bike or rely on its insurance without one. The only riding allowed without an MOT is to a pre-booked test, or to or from somewhere for repairs. Our guide to driving without an MOT covers the exceptions and the penalties.

Class 1, class 2 — and what they cost

Motorcycles are split into two MOT classes by engine size. Class 1 covers motorcycles up to 200cc — that includes mopeds and 125s. Class 2 covers motorcycles over 200cc. The test itself is the same for both, and so is the price: the maximum fee a test centre can charge is £29.65 for a solo bike in either class, and £37.80 if a sidecar is fitted. There is no VAT on the fee, and centres can charge less than the maximum.

The class matters for one practical reason: a test centre must be authorised for your bike's class and have the equipment to test bikes. A car-only centre cannot do a motorcycle MOT, and not every bike centre is set up for large machines. Check when you book, especially for a big bike or one with a sidecar. Our MOT cost guide shows the full official fee table for every vehicle class.

What the tester checks on a motorcycle

The official GOV.UK list of motorcycle parts checked at an MOT runs to sixteen items. Several of them have no equivalent on a car test at all:

Lights and indicators. Headlamp, sidelights, rear light, registration plate light, indicators and reflectors — that they work, are secure and are the right colour — plus headlamp aim. Indicators must flash at the right rate.

Steering and suspension. Forks, handlebars, grips and mountings, head bearings, swinging arm and shock absorbers — secure, undamaged, and with proper damping. Worn head bearings and leaking fork seals are common bike failures.

Wheels and tyres. Condition, security, correct size and fitment, tread depth and valve condition. The minimum legal tread depth for a motorcycle is 1.0 mm across at least three-quarters of the breadth of the tread — not the 1.6 mm figure used for cars, which is the mistake most general guides make.

Frame. Free from cracks, and checked for damage, distortion and corrosion that could affect steering or braking. On an older bike, corrosion around the steering head and swingarm pivot is what the tester looks for.

Brakes. Condition, operation and performance on the efficiency test, plus the levers and pedal. Both brakes are tested — a bike that only really stops on one brake will fail.

Exhaust, fuel system and noise. The exhaust must be secure and complete, and not too noisy. The fuel system is checked for leaks and for the security of its components, including the cap and hoses.

Chain and sprocket. The drive chain must not be too loose, too tight or worn, the sprockets must not be too worn, and the chain guard must be secure. This is a bike-only check, and a slack or rusted chain is one of the easiest failures to avoid: adjust and lubricate it before the test.

The rest. Wheel alignment front to rear, the seat present and secure, footrests present and secure, throttle working properly, clutch lever not so bent, damaged or shortened that it cannot be operated easily, horn working, and registration plates, vehicle identification and frame numbers present and legible. If a sidecar is fitted, its attachment, alignment, suspension, wheel bearings, wheel alignment, lights and tyres are checked too.

What is not in the test is the same as for cars: the condition of the engine, clutch and gearbox is not checked, and the MOT is not a service. The clutch lever is checked, but the clutch itself is not — a distinction GOV.UK makes explicitly.

No emissions test for motorcycles

Here is the fact that surprises most riders, and the one most guides get wrong. A motorcycle MOT has no exhaust emissions test. The DVSA motorcycle inspection manual has no emissions section at all — its section 8 covers exhaust noise, not emissions — and the official parts checklist mentions the exhaust only for security and noise. The tester revs the engine with it warm and judges the noise: an exhaust that is unreasonably loud for the bike, or insecure and likely to come off, is a fail. But no probe goes up the tailpipe, and there is no smoke check like the one on a car test.

That does not mean you can fit any exhaust you like. An aftermarket can that is stamped "not for road use" or that makes the bike far louder than standard is exactly what the noise check is there to catch. If you have changed the exhaust, keep the original, and be honest with yourself about how loud the bike is before you present it.

Five quick checks before the test

Bikes fail on small, cheap things far more often than on anything serious. A few minutes the day before the test covers most of them:

Lights. Switch everything on — headlamp main and dip, tail light, brake light from both the lever and the pedal, indicators both sides. A blown bulb is the most avoidable failure there is.

Tyres. Check the tread is at least 1 mm deep across most of its width, look for cuts and cracking on the sidewalls, and set the pressures. Tyres that have stood all winter can look fine and still be cracked.

Chain. Check the tension against the handbook figure, lubricate it, and look at the sprocket teeth — hooked or sharply pointed teeth mean the sprocket is worn.

Brakes. Squeeze both levers and press the pedal. They should feel firm, not spongy, and the pads should have material left. Top up the brake fluid if it is low, and look for leaks around the calipers.

Horn, mirrors and plate. Sound the horn, check the mirrors are secure and give a view, and clean the number plate so it is legible. A dirty plate is a silly reason to fail.

If the bike fails, the result is recorded the same way as for a car, and you can check it — along with the full history of every past test — free on GOV.UK. Our guide to how to check MOT history shows how, using just the registration number.

Quick answers

How much does a motorcycle MOT cost?

The maximum fee is £29.65 for a motorcycle in class 1 or class 2, and £37.80 if a sidecar is fitted. Test centres can charge less, and there is no VAT on the fee.

When does a motorcycle need its first MOT?

By the third anniversary of its registration, and then every year after that. The rule is the same for mopeds, scooters and large motorcycles.

Do motorcycles get an emissions test at the MOT?

No. The motorcycle MOT has no exhaust emissions test — the DVSA motorcycle inspection manual has no emissions section at all. The exhaust is checked for security and noise only.

What is the minimum tyre tread depth for a motorcycle MOT?

1.0 mm, measured across at least three-quarters of the breadth of the tread. That is less than the 1.6 mm minimum for cars, so do not use the car figure when checking a bike.

What is the difference between a class 1 and class 2 motorcycle MOT?

Class 1 covers motorcycles up to 200cc, including mopeds and 125s; class 2 covers motorcycles over 200cc. The test and the maximum fee are the same for both — the class only matters when you choose a test centre, because it must be authorised for your bike's class.

To sum up: a motorcycle MOT costs at most £29.65 (£37.80 with a sidecar), is due from three years old and every year after, and checks sixteen items from lights and brakes to the chain and sprocket — with no emissions test. Check the lights, the 1 mm tread, the chain and the brakes before you go, and make sure the centre you book is authorised for bikes.
