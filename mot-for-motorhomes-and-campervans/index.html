MOT for Motorhomes and Campervans: Classes, Costs and What Gets Checked

Last reviewed: November 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

Booking an MOT for a motorhome causes more confusion than it should. Owners are told their vehicle needs a class 7 test, an HGV test, or a special motorhome test that costs extra, usually by websites that have mixed motorhomes up with vans. In reality, a motorhome MOT is a standard class 4 test, the same class as a family car, with the same maximum fee. This guide explains the rules as GOV.UK sets them out, what the tester actually looks at, and the few things that genuinely are different when the vehicle is also your holiday home. What class of MOT does a motorhome need?

Motor caravans are class 4. That is not a rule of thumb, it is how GOV.UK lists them in the official MOT fees table, where "motor caravans" appear as their own row in class 4, alongside cars with up to 8 passenger seats. The maximum fee a test centre can charge is £54.85, exactly the same as a car, and there is no VAT on the test fee. A centre can charge less than the maximum, and some do.

Class follows what the vehicle is, not what it started life as or how much it happens to weigh. A small campervan, a coachbuilt motorhome and a large A-class are all motor caravans, so all of them take a class 4 test. The one exception to keep in mind is seating: vehicles built to carry 13 or more passengers fall into class 5 instead, so if your motorhome is a minibus conversion that still has 13 or more passenger seats fitted, mention it when you book and let the centre confirm the class.

The weight myth: heavy motorhomes, class 7 and HGV tests

The most common mistake in motorhome MOT advice is the claim that a motorhome over 3,000kg needs a class 7 test, or an HGV test over 3,500kg. Those weight bands are real, but they belong to goods vehicles. In the same GOV.UK fees table, class 7 is defined as petrol, diesel and hybrid goods vehicles over 3,000kg up to 3,500kg design gross weight, vans and trucks carrying loads, not motor caravans. Lorries get an annual HGV test instead of an MOT, but a motorhome is not a lorry, however big it is.

So a 3,850kg motorhome still takes a class 4 MOT at a normal test centre. The genuine problem with large motorhomes is physical, not legal: not every centre has a ramp, lift or brake tester that a long, tall or heavy motorhome fits on, and some centres simply cannot take them. When you book, give the centre the vehicle's length, height and weight and ask outright whether it fits their equipment. It saves a wasted trip on the day. When is a motorhome's first MOT due?

The timetable is the same as for a car. The first MOT is due by the third anniversary of the vehicle's registration, and after that it needs a test every year. You can have the test done up to a month (minus a day) before the current certificate runs out and keep the same renewal date for the following year, which is handy if the MOT falls in the middle of your touring season, get it done early rather than trying to find a test centre while you are away.

If you have bought a used motorhome and don't know where you stand, check its record before you book anything. The official GOV.UK service shows the expiry date and the full test history from just the registration number, and our guide to when your MOT is due explains the dates and the early-testing rule in more detail.

What the tester checks: and what they don't

A motorhome is tested on the same vehicle items as a car: lights, brakes, tyres, steering, suspension, exhaust emissions, seatbelts, mirrors, the windscreen and wipers, and the structure of the vehicle for corrosion and damage. As with cars, the test does not cover the condition of the engine, clutch or gearbox. If it is part of the vehicle and it affects safety or emissions, it is in the test; the tester works through the same inspection standards used for any class 4 vehicle.

What is not in the test is the part that makes it a motorhome. The cooker, fridge, gas system, water system, leisure battery and the rest of the living area equipment are not examined, because the MOT is about the vehicle, not the accommodation. Checking those is the job of a habitation service, which dealers and mobile engineers offer, worth doing for your own safety, especially for gas, but it is optional and has nothing to do with passing the MOT.

Two weak spots catch motorhomes out more than cars. The first is tyres: a motorhome that stands parked for months can have tyres that look fine on tread but are cracked on the sidewalls from age and standing, and testers look for exactly that. The second is corrosion and damp-related damage around the chassis and floor on older vehicles. Running through our pre-MOT checklist a week or two before the test gives you time to sort the cheap items, bulbs, wipers, tyre pressures, before the tester finds them.

Campervan conversions

If you have converted a panel van into a campervan yourself, the MOT position is straightforward: the tester tests the vehicle in front of them, and a van fitted out and used as a motor caravan is booked in and tested as one. Tell the centre it is a campervan conversion when you book, partly so they class it correctly and partly so they can allow for its size and weight. Separately, if the log book (V5C) still describes the vehicle as a panel van, you can apply to the DVLA to have the body type updated to motor caravan, that is a registration matter, not something the MOT centre decides on the day.

Old motorhomes and the 40-year exemption

Classic motorhomes get the same treatment as classic cars. Vehicles over 40 years old may not need an MOT at all, provided they have not been substantially changed, a period campervan that is still essentially original can qualify. The exemption never excuses an unroadworthy vehicle: even an exempt motorhome must be safe every time it is driven. Our guide to MOT exemptions explains the 40-year rule and what counts as a substantial change.

Quick answers

What MOT class is a motorhome?

Class 4, the same class as an ordinary car. GOV.UK lists motor caravans in class 4 in its official fees table, with a maximum test fee of £54.85. The class follows what the vehicle is, a motor caravan, rather than how much it weighs.

My motorhome weighs over 3,500kg. Does it need a class 7 or HGV test?

No. Class 7 covers goods vehicles with a design gross weight over 3,000kg up to 3,500kg, and HGV tests are for lorries. A motor caravan stays in class 4 however heavy it is. The real issue with a big motorhome is practical: check when you book that the centre has a ramp or lift that fits it.

When is a motorhome's first MOT due?

By the third anniversary of its registration, the same as a car, and then every year after that. You can check the exact expiry date free on the official GOV.UK MOT history service using just the registration number.

Does the MOT check the living area, gas and water systems?

No. The MOT covers the vehicle itself, brakes, lights, tyres, steering, suspension, emissions and structure. The cooker, fridge, gas system, water and leisure electrics in the living area are not part of the test. A habitation service checks those, but it is a separate, optional service, not a legal requirement.

Is an old motorhome exempt from the MOT?

It can be. Vehicles over 40 years old may not need an MOT if they have not been substantially changed, and classic motorhomes can qualify on the same basis as classic cars. The vehicle still has to be roadworthy whenever it is driven.

To sum up: a motorhome MOT is a class 4 test at a maximum of £54.85, due from three years old and every year after, covering the vehicle and not the living area. Book a centre that can physically fit your motorhome, ignore anyone who tells you it needs a goods vehicle test because of its weight, and your motorhome MOT becomes as routine as a car's.
