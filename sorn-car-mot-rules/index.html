SORN Cars and MOT: The Rules Before You Drive Again

Last reviewed: October 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

If your car has been declared off the road with a SORN, it does not need a current MOT while it stays there. The catch is that you can't use it on a public road at all — not even for a short trip. This guide explains what SORN means, the one journey you are allowed to make, and the exact order of steps to get the car legal again.

What SORN means

SORN stands for Statutory Off Road Notification. You tell DVLA your vehicle is off the road, and in return you don't have to tax it or keep it insured while it stays there. People use a SORN for cars that are laid up over winter, waiting for repairs, or kept as a project.

A few basics worth knowing:

  • Making a SORN is free, and you do it with DVLA through GOV.UK.
  • You get a refund of vehicle tax for any full months remaining.
  • A SORN does not expire and does not need renewing. It lasts until you tax the vehicle again, or sell, scrap or permanently export it — at which point it is cancelled automatically.

Does a SORN car need an MOT?

No. While the car is on a SORN and kept off the road, it does not need a valid MOT, tax or insurance. If the MOT runs out during that time, nothing bad happens — you have not broken any rule, because the car is not being used.

The MOT only matters again when you want to drive the car. At that point the order of events matters, because you cannot tax a car that needs an MOT without a current pass. More on that below.

One warning: these rules only protect you while the car genuinely stays off the road. A SORN car sitting on the street outside your house is not off the road in the eyes of the law.

Where you can keep a SORN car

The car must be kept on private land — in a garage, on a driveway, or on private land such as a field or yard. It must not be kept or parked on a public road, even if you never drive it. Leaving it on the street "just for a few days" breaks the rules, and DVLA uses number plate recognition cameras to spot untaxed vehicles.

If the land belongs to someone else, get the owner's permission first. A public car park or a pavement does not count as off-road storage, even if it feels out of the way.

The one journey you are allowed: a pre-booked MOT

There is a single exception to the no-driving rule. You can drive a SORN car on a public road to go to or from a pre-booked MOT or other testing appointment. GOV.UK is clear that this is the only reason you may use it on the road.

To stay legal on that journey:

  • The test must be genuinely booked in advance. Driving to the garage to "arrange something" does not count.
  • Drive straight there and back — no detours, no shopping on the way.
  • The car must be insured for the trip. A SORN removes the need for insurance while the car is off the road, but driving on a public road without insurance is always an offence, so arrange cover before you set off, even if it is a short-term policy.
  • The car still needs to be in a safe condition. If it has a dangerous fault, don't drive it — have it transported instead.

Use the car on the road for anything else and you could face court prosecution and a fine of up to £2,500. Note that trips to a garage for repairs are not covered by this exception — it is for the test appointment itself.

Bringing a SORN car back onto the road

When you are ready to use the car again, there is no "un-SORN" form to fill in. Taxing the vehicle cancels the SORN automatically. Do things in this order:

  1. Insurance first. You need cover in place before the car turns a wheel on a public road, including the trip to the test centre.
  2. Then the MOT. Book the test and drive the car there under the exception above. Most cars over three years old need a valid MOT before they can be taxed, so the pass has to come first. If the car fails, our guide on what to do if your car fails its MOT explains your options for repairs and retests. Costs are capped — see our page on MOT cost for the maximum fees.
  3. Then tax it. Once you have insurance and a valid MOT, tax the vehicle online with the reference number from your V5C log book. The moment the tax starts, the SORN ends and the car is fully legal again.

Until all three are in place, the car stays off the road.

Older and classic cars

Some older vehicles do not need an MOT at all. Vehicles built or first registered more than 40 years ago are usually exempt, as long as they have not been substantially changed. If that applies to your laid-up classic, taxing it is simpler — read our guide to MOT exemptions for the full rules before you rely on it.

Quick answers

Can I park a SORN car on the road if I don't drive it?

No. A SORN vehicle must be kept off public roads completely — parked on the street is still breaking the rules, and the car can be clamped or removed.

Do I need insurance while my car is on a SORN?

Not by law, as long as it stays off the road. Some owners keep a basic "laid up" policy to cover fire or theft while the car is stored, but that is a choice, not a legal duty. You must have proper road cover before driving it anywhere, including to an MOT.

Can I drive a SORN car to a garage for repairs?

No. The only driving allowed is to or from a pre-booked MOT or other testing appointment. If the car needs repairs first, have it transported, or get the repairs done after it passes its MOT.

My MOT expired while the car was on a SORN. Is that a problem?

No. An expired MOT only becomes an issue when you want to use the car again. Book a test, drive there under the pre-booked appointment rule, and tax the car once it passes.

Does a SORN run out if I forget about it?

No. A SORN has no expiry date and never needs renewing. It ends only when you tax the vehicle, or when it is sold, scrapped or exported.

Before you drive any car again, you can check its tax and MOT status free on the official GOV.UK service — it takes a minute and confirms everything is in order.
