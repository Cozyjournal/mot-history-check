Diesel Car MOT: The Emissions Test, the DPF Check and What Fails

Last reviewed: November 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

A diesel car MOT is the same test as a petrol MOT for most of the car — brakes, tyres, lights, suspension, steering and structure are all checked the same way. The difference is the exhaust. Instead of the petrol gas test, a diesel gets a smoke test measured with a meter, and its exhaust filter gets its own checks. Most guides online get the details wrong, from the smoke limits to what happens if the filter has been removed. This guide follows the DVSA MOT inspection manual, section 8.2.2, so every figure below is the one the tester actually uses.

How a diesel car MOT is different

A diesel car needs its first MOT by the third anniversary of its registration, and then one every year — the same as any car. The fee is the same too: a class 4 test with a maximum of £54.85, and no VAT on the fee. Our MOT cost guide shows the full official fee table.

The difference is only in the emissions section of the test. A petrol car gets a gas test: a probe in the tailpipe measures carbon monoxide and hydrocarbons at idle and at fast idle. A diesel gets a smoke test instead, also called an opacity test: a meter measures how much light the exhaust smoke blocks while the tester revs the engine. Everything else — the sixteen-plus categories from brakes to bodywork — is identical. If you are not sure when yours is due, check the date free on the official GOV.UK MOT history service using just the registration number — our guide to when your MOT is due explains the dates and the early-testing rule.

The smoke test: how it works and the limits

The smoke test is done with the engine at its normal operating temperature — at least 80°C — because a cold diesel smokes more and the reading would be meaningless. The tester puts a probe from an approved diesel smoke meter into the exhaust, warms the engine through, then presses the accelerator quickly and continuously so the engine reaches full fuel position in under a second. The meter reads the smoke level after each acceleration. If the first reading is above the limit, the tester does two more accelerations and takes the mean; if the mean is still above the limit, up to six accelerations in total can be done.

The limit the car is tested against comes first from the manufacturer's plate — usually on the VIN plate or a sticker under the bonnet, often marked with a number like 0.5. The tester adds 0.05 to the plate value when entering it on the smoke tester, to allow for manufacturing tolerances. Where there is no plate value, the DVSA manual sets default limits by the car's age:

First used before 1 July 2008: 2.5 m-1 for a non-turbo engine, or 3.0 m-1 for a turbo engine.

First used between 1 July 2008 and 31 December 2013: 1.5 m-1 for all engines.

First used on or after 1 January 2014: 0.7 m-1 for all engines.

Newer diesels get far less leeway — a car first used from 2014 is allowed 0.7, less than a quarter of the 3.0 an older turbo diesel gets. A smoke reading above the limit is a major fault, and one major fault is all it takes to fail. The car passes the opacity test if the first acceleration shows a smoke level at 60% or less of the limit, or if the mean of the first three readings — or of any consecutive three readings — is at or below the limit.

The tester will not do the smoke test at all if the engine is not in a safe condition — too little oil, low oil pressure, abnormal engine noise, a tampered governor or a camshaft belt in poor condition. In that case the test is refused and the reason is recorded. That is another reason to check the oil before you go, not after.

The DPF check

The diesel particulate filter, or DPF, is the filter in the exhaust that traps soot. It has been part of the MOT since February 2014, when the exhaust check started to include making sure the filter is there if the car was built with one. The tester checks only what is visible and identifiable — the filter itself, the diesel oxidation catalyst, the exhaust gas recirculation valve and the selective catalytic reduction system — and three things about the DPF can fail the car:

Missing. If the car was built with a DPF and it is not there, that is a major fault. Emission control equipment fitted by the manufacturer that is missing, obviously modified or obviously defective is a major fault under the manual.

Tampered with. Evidence that the DPF has been tampered with is a major fault in its own right. A DPF can that has clearly been cut open and rewelded is rejected, unless the person presenting the car can show a valid reason for cutting it open, such as cleaning the filter.

Visible smoke. On a car fitted with a DPF, the tester also watches the exhaust during the metered check. Visible smoke of any colour from the exhaust of a DPF-equipped car is a major fault — it does not matter how faint it is or what colour it is. This rule has applied since May 2018, and it is the one that catches the most modified diesels. Our modified car MOT guide covers decat exhausts, DPF deletes and the other modifications that fail.

A blocked DPF is a subtler case. The tester does not measure how full the filter is, and there is no MOT test for soot inside the filter. A badly blocked filter can still pass if nothing the tester has to check shows up on the day. But a blocked filter often brings on the engine warning light or pushes the smoke reading over the limit — and either of those is a fail.

The engine warning light

The engine malfunction indicator lamp — the engine warning light — is checked on diesel cars first used on or after 1 July 2008. The tester switches the ignition on and checks that the light comes on and then goes off. A lamp that is inoperative, or that stays on indicating a malfunction, is a major fault.

This is the check that most often fails a diesel before the smoke test even starts. A DPF warning light on the dashboard is not itself listed separately in the manual, but a badly blocked filter will often bring on the engine warning light as well — so if either light is on when you arrive, get it investigated first. Driving to the test with a warning light on and hoping it goes out is not a plan.

Removing a DPF is illegal

This is where the MOT rules and the law on the road meet. The Department for Transport's guidance note on diesel particulate filters says that removing a DPF will almost always break regulation 61A of the Road Vehicles (Construction and Use) Regulations 1986, which covers emissions from vehicles in use. The penalties are fines of up to £1,000 for a car and up to £2,500 for a light goods vehicle.

That matters because the MOT is only a check on one day. A car that passes its MOT with a modified exhaust can still be illegal to drive every other day of the year. The same applies to the other emissions equipment: a missing catalytic converter, a deleted EGR valve or a disabled AdBlue system falls under the same rule — emission control equipment fitted by the manufacturer that is missing, obviously modified or obviously defective is a major fault at the test, and the vehicle is illegal on the road either way. If a garage offers you a DPF delete, it is offering you a fail and a fine, not a fix.

Diesel hybrids skip the smoke test

There is one exception to the smoke test, and it surprises diesel drivers who have switched to a hybrid. The DVSA manual says testers do not need to carry out the smoke test on hybrid vehicles, including mild hybrids, with electric and combustion engines. So a diesel hybrid does not get the metered smoke test. The engine warning light is still checked on a diesel hybrid first used on or after 1 July 2008, and the visual smoke check still applies — if the exhaust gives off dense blue or clearly visible black smoke, the car fails. Five checks before a diesel MOT

Diesels fail on the emissions section more often than petrol cars fail on theirs, and most of it is preventable. A few things before the test make a real difference:

Give it a proper run first. A diesel that has only done short trips for weeks will have a DPF full of soot and an engine that never got hot. Take it for a steady run of 20 to 30 minutes at motorway or dual-carriageway speed before the test, so the filter gets a chance to regenerate and the engine is properly warm when the tester starts. Arrive with the engine hot — the smoke test needs at least 80°C.

Check the oil. The tester will not run the smoke test if the oil is too low or the pressure is too low. Check the level the day before, and top it up if it needs it.

Fix the warning lights. Any engine warning light or DPF warning light on the dashboard is a fail waiting to happen. Get it diagnosed before the test, not after it.

Look at the exhaust. Start the car when it is warm and watch the tailpipe while someone revs the engine. Visible smoke of any colour on a DPF car is a major fault, so if you can see smoke, the tester will too.

Do the usual checks too. The emissions section is only one part of the test. Run through our pre-MOT checklist — lights, tyre pressures and tread, wipers, washer fluid, horn — and read the most common MOT failure reasons so you know what else the tester will be looking at.

If the car fails, the result is recorded the same way as for any car, and you can check it — along with the full history of every past test — free on GOV.UK. Quick answers

Do diesel cars get an emissions test at the MOT?

Yes. A diesel gets a smoke test — also called an opacity test — instead of the petrol gas test. A meter measures how much light the exhaust smoke blocks while the tester revs the warm engine.

What is the smoke limit for a diesel car MOT?

The limit on the manufacturer's plate comes first — usually on the VIN plate or a sticker under the bonnet. Where there is no plate value, the default limits are 2.5 m-1 for a non-turbo or 3.0 m-1 for a turbo engine first used before 1 July 2008, 1.5 m-1 for cars first used between 1 July 2008 and 31 December 2013, and 0.7 m-1 for cars first used on or after 1 January 2014.

Can a missing or removed DPF fail an MOT?

Yes. A car built with a diesel particulate filter fails its MOT if the filter is missing, and evidence that it has been tampered with is a major fault. Removing a DPF from a vehicle used on public roads is also illegal, with fines of up to £1,000 for a car.

Does a diesel hybrid get a smoke test?

No. Diesel hybrids, including mild hybrids, skip the metered smoke test. The tester still does a visual smoke check and still checks the engine warning light.

Will my diesel fail its MOT if the DPF warning light is on?

A warning light that stays on is a fail. The engine malfunction indicator lamp is checked on diesels first used on or after 1 July 2008, and a lamp that is inoperative or indicating a malfunction is a major fault. Get any warning light investigated before the test.

What smoke limit is used if the manufacturer’s plate shows no value?

The DVSA manual’s default limits apply, based on when the vehicle was first used. Before 1 July 2008: 2.5 m-1 for a non-turbocharged engine, or 3.0 m-1 for a turbocharged engine. From 1 July 2008 to 31 December 2013: 1.5 m-1 for all engines. On or after 1 January 2014: 0.7 m-1 for all engines. Where a plate value exists, the tester uses that instead and adds 0.05 for tolerance.

To sum up: a diesel car MOT is the same class 4 test as a petrol car, at a maximum of £54.85, but the emissions section is a smoke test with a meter, not a gas test. The limit comes from the plate on your car, or 0.7 m-1 for cars first used from 2014. The DPF must be there and untouched, visible smoke of any colour on a DPF car is a major fault, and the engine warning light must work and stay off. Give the car a proper run before the test, arrive with the engine hot, and fix any warning light first.
