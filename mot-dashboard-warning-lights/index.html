MOT Warning Lights: Which Dashboard Lights Fail the Test

Last reviewed: November 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

Not every dashboard warning light fails an MOT — but several do, and they are the ones drivers most often ignore. During the test, the tester switches on the ignition and watches each warning lamp: it should come on, then go out once the engine is running. A lamp that stays on to show a fault, or one that never comes on at all, can be a major defect — and a major defect means a fail. This guide lists every warning light the MOT checks, which ones fail and on which cars, and which lights you can safely ignore until after the test.

The lights that fail — at a glance

On cars the check applies to, a warning lamp showing a malfunction in any of these systems is a major defect:

Engine management light (MIL). Major — petrol cars first used from 1 July 2003, diesel cars from 1 July 2008.

ABS warning light. Major — the warning device does not work, or it shows a system malfunction.

Airbag and SRS light. Major — the lamp shows a fault with the airbag or supplementary restraint system.

Electronic stability control (ESC). Major — but only when the tester is sure the lamp means an ESC malfunction, not that the system has simply been switched off.

Tyre pressure monitoring system (TPMS). Major — on cars first used on or after 1 January 2012, when the system itself is faulty or plainly not working.

Electronic parking brake. Major — the warning light shows a malfunction.

Electronic power steering. Major — the warning light shows a fault.

The dates matter: the engine light and TPMS checks only apply to cars first used after the dates above. An older car with no TPMS, or a petrol car from before July 2003, is not failed on a light it was never required to have.

Engine management light (MIL)

The engine management light — the check-engine light — is checked as part of the emissions section of the test. For petrol cars, including hybrids, first used on or after 1 July 2003, and for diesel cars first used on or after 1 July 2008, a lamp that is inoperative or indicating a malfunction is a major defect. The same dates apply to gas and bi-fuel cars from 1 July 2008.

Two things catch people out. First, a light that has been removed or disabled to hide a fault fails just as surely as one that is showing a fault — the tester checks that the lamp works. Second, the light can be on for a fault that has nothing to do with emissions on the day: a misfire, a faulty sensor or an exhaust problem all log a fault, and the lamp stays on until the fault is fixed and cleared. Get the fault code read before the test, not after a fail. Our diesel car MOT guide explains how the same light is checked on diesels.

ABS warning light

If the ABS warning light is on, the anti-lock braking system is not working properly — and that is a major defect. The tester checks that the warning device operates when the ignition is switched on, and that it goes out once the engine is running. A lamp that never lights up is treated the same way as one that stays lit: either way the warning system is not working, and the car fails.

An ABS light can mean anything from a wheel-speed sensor to damaged wiring. The brakes themselves usually still work, but the wheels can lock in an emergency stop, which is why the test treats it as a safety failure rather than an advisory. Do not book the test hoping the light will go out on the drive over — if the fault is still logged, it will still be lit.

Airbag and SRS warning light

A lit airbag light means the supplementary restraint system has a fault, and the airbags may not deploy in a crash. The DVSA manual classes a lamp indicating an SRS malfunction as a major defect, so the car fails.

There is one exception that confuses people: a lamp showing that the passenger airbag has been switched off is not a defect. That is the car working as designed — for example when a child seat is fitted in the front — not a fault. The tester is checking for a system malfunction, not for a feature being used properly. Seatbelt pretensioners and load limiters are checked under the same system, so a fault in those shows up as an SRS light too.

Electronic stability control (ESC)

Stability control goes by many names — ESC, ESP, DSC — but the MOT rule is the same for all of them: a warning lamp indicating an ESC malfunction is a major defect. The manual tells testers to fail the car only when they are certain the lamp means a fault, because a lamp can also show that the driver has switched the system off, which is normal. If you have turned ESC off, switch it back on before the test so there is nothing for the tester to wonder about.

Tyre pressure monitoring system (TPMS)

This is the light most drivers misunderstand. On cars first used on or after 1 January 2012, a TPMS that is malfunctioning or obviously not working is a major defect — the system itself has failed. But a light that is on simply because a tyre is low is not a system failure. Check the pressures, top the low tyre up, and reset the system. If the light goes out, the system is working and there is nothing to fail.

One useful clue: if the TPMS light flashes for a minute or so and then stays on, the system itself is faulty — often a dead sensor battery. That is the pattern that fails. A steady light after a puncture or a cold morning is usually just low pressure.

Electronic parking brake and power steering

Electronic parking brake. If its warning light shows a malfunction, that is a major defect. The tester checks that the brake holds the car and releases properly, and a fault light means the system cannot be trusted to do either.

Electronic power steering. A warning light showing a fault with the power steering is a major defect. Heavy steering on its own is not necessarily a fail, but a lit warning lamp is — it means the system has logged a fault the tester cannot ignore.

The lights that do not fail on their own

Brake fluid warning light. A warning light that is on, or does not work properly, is a minor defect. Minor defects are recorded on the certificate but the car still passes. Check the fluid level anyway — low fluid usually means worn pads or a leak, and either of those can become a fail.

Brake pad wear light. Not a reason to fail on its own. What fails is pads that have worn down to the indicator, which the tester checks directly by looking at the pads.

Passenger airbag off. As above, not a defect — it shows a feature being used, not a fault.

Low fuel, washer fluid, service reminders. None of these are part of the MOT. A service reminder light is not a defect, and neither is a low washer-fluid warning — although the washers themselves must work, so top the bottle up.

What to do if a dashboard warning light is on before your MOT

Do not book the test and hope. Work through this first:

Read the fault code. A cheap code reader, or a garage diagnostic, tells you what the light means. A loose fuel cap can bring on the engine light; a wheel sensor can bring on the ABS light. Knowing the code turns a mystery into a repair with a price.

Fix it, then drive the car. Clearing a code without fixing the fault does not help — the light comes back as soon as the car runs its self-check, often during the test itself. Fix the fault, then drive normally for a day or two so the system can confirm the repair.

Check the simple things. Tyre pressures for the TPMS light, brake fluid level for the brake warning, and whether ESC has simply been switched off.

Quick answers

Does the engine management light fail an MOT?

Yes, on cars it applies to. An engine malfunction indicator lamp that is inoperative or indicating a malfunction is a major fault on petrol cars first used on or after 1 July 2003, and on diesel cars first used on or after 1 July 2008. A major fault means the car fails.

Does the ABS warning light fail an MOT?

Yes. An ABS warning device that does not operate, or that indicates a system malfunction, is a major defect. The tester checks it by switching on the ignition and watching the lamp come on and then go out once the engine is running.

Does the tyre pressure warning light fail an MOT?

Only if the tyre pressure monitoring system itself is faulty. On cars first used on or after 1 January 2012, a TPMS that is malfunctioning or obviously not working is a major defect. A light that is on simply because a tyre is low is not a system failure — top the tyre up and reset the system.

Does the airbag warning light fail an MOT?

Yes, if it shows a fault with the airbag or supplementary restraint system. A lamp showing that the passenger airbag has been switched off is not a defect — that is the car working as designed, not a fault.

Can I pass an MOT with a warning light on?

It depends on the light. A brake fluid warning light is a minor defect, so the car can still pass, and a brake pad wear light is not a reason to fail on its own. But engine management, ABS, airbag, stability control, power steering, electronic parking brake and faulty TPMS lights are major defects — the car fails.

To sum up: a dashboard warning light fails an MOT when it shows a fault in a system the test checks — engine management, ABS, airbag, stability control, power steering, electronic parking brake, or a faulty tyre pressure system on cars from 2012. A brake fluid light is only a minor defect, and a low-tyre light is not a fail at all. Check the dashboard after the engine starts, before you book the test — a light that stays on is a fail waiting to happen, and fixing it first is always cheaper than a fail and a retest.
