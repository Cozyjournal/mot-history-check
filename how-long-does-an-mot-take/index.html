How Long Does an MOT Take? The 45–60 Minute Test Explained

Last reviewed: November 2026 • Published by MOT History Check

A standard car MOT takes 45 to 60 minutes. That is the inspection itself, the time the tester spends working through the DVSA checklist, from the lights and tyres to the brakes, suspension and emissions. What catches most drivers out is that the test time and the garage time are not the same thing: a 45-minute test does not mean you get your car back in 45 minutes. This guide explains what happens during that hour, how long each type of vehicle takes, what can make it run longer, and how long a retest takes if your car fails. Ready to get it done? The short answer: 45 to 60 minutes for a car

For a standard car, a class 4 vehicle, which covers most cars with up to eight passenger seats, the MOT takes 45 to 60 minutes. The RAC gives the same figure, and it is the number nearly every garage in the country quotes. The tester has to inspect dozens of safety and emissions items, so a proper test simply cannot be done in 15 or 20 minutes. If a garage offers you a ten-minute MOT, that is a warning sign, not a bargain.

That 45 to 60 minutes is active inspection time. It does not include waiting for a free ramp, the paperwork at the start and end, or any repairs. So when you book, plan around the test time but expect to be without the car for longer, often a morning, and sometimes the whole day if you drop it off. If you are not sure when your test is due in the first place, check the date free on the official GOV.UK MOT history service, our guide to when your MOT is due explains how, and the rule that lets you test up to a month early without losing your renewal date.

What happens during those 45–60 minutes

The tester works through the DVSA inspection manual in a set order, and the test has a few clear phases. Knowing them helps you understand where the hour goes:

Outside the car. The tester checks the lights, indicators, number plates, windscreen, wipers and washers, mirrors, doors, bonnet and boot, and the bodywork for sharp edges or dangerous corrosion. The tyres are checked for tread depth, damage and correct pressures, and the wheels for condition and security.

Under the car. On the ramp, the tester inspects the brakes, suspension, steering, exhaust and fuel system, and the underside of the body and chassis for corrosion. The brake test itself is done on a roller brake tester, which measures how well each wheel brakes and whether the brakes are balanced across each axle.

Inside the car. Seatbelts, seats, the horn, the steering wheel, the dashboard warning lights and the driver's view of the road are all checked. The tester also checks the vehicle identification number against the records.

Emissions. A petrol car gets a gas test with a probe in the tailpipe; a diesel gets a smoke test with a meter. This is the part that takes the longest to set up, because the engine has to be at its normal operating temperature, at least 80°C for a diesel, before the reading means anything. Our guides to diesel car MOTs and hybrid car MOTs explain how the emissions section differs for those cars.

Paperwork. The result is recorded on the DVSA system, and the tester gives you the outcome: a pass certificate, or a refusal with the defects listed. You can check the result, and the full history of every past test, free on GOV.UK; our guide to how to check MOT history shows how, using just the registration number.

How long an MOT takes by vehicle type

The 45–60 minute figure is for a standard car. Other vehicles take a different amount of time, because there is more, or less, to check:

Motorcycle (class 1 and 2): around 30 to 45 minutes. There are fewer items to check than on a car, and no emissions test in the same form. The maximum fee is £29.65 for a standard motorcycle. Our motorcycle MOT guide covers what gets checked and the class system in full.

Car and small van (class 4): 45 to 60 minutes. This covers most cars, motorhomes up to eight passenger seats, and small vans. The maximum fee is £54.85.

Larger van (class 7): around 60 to 90 minutes. A van between 3,000kg and 3,500kg has heavier braking and load-bearing parts to check, so the test runs longer. The maximum fee is £58.60. Our van MOT guide explains the class 4 versus class 7 split.

Minibus and larger motorhome (class 5): 45 to 90 minutes, depending on the number of seats and whether a seatbelt installation check is needed. The maximum fee runs from £59.55 up to £124.50 for the largest vehicles with the seatbelt check. Our motorhome MOT guide covers the class rules for motorhomes.

These are typical times, and they vary from garage to garage. The DVSA does not set a fixed time limit, the tester has to complete every check in the manual, and a careful tester on an older car will take longer than a quick one on a nearly-new car. The fee, by contrast, is fixed: our MOT cost guide lists the maximum fee for every vehicle class, and no test centre may charge more than the maximum.

What can make an MOT take longer

A test that should take an hour can stretch well past it. The usual reasons are:

Faults found during the test. If the tester finds a problem, they have to record it properly and, in many cases, show you or explain it. A car that fails still takes the full 45 to 60 minutes, the tester works through the whole checklist either way, because a fail on one item does not stop the rest of the test.

Repairs on the spot. Many garages will fix small things during the test, a bulb, a wiper blade, a tyre pressure, and that time adds on. Bigger repairs mean the car stays in the workshop after the test, and you may not get it back until the end of the day.

A busy garage. Your appointment time is not the same as your test time. If the garage is running behind, your car may sit in the yard for an hour before the tester even starts. Saturday mornings and the weeks around the end of the month are the busiest times.

An MOT and a service together. A combined booking is convenient, but it is a much longer appointment. An interim service takes around 1 to 1.5 hours on its own, and a full service up to 90 minutes, so an MOT plus a full service is roughly 2.5 to 3 hours in total. Book the two together only if you can leave the car for the morning.

A cold engine. The emissions test needs a warm engine. If you arrive after a five-minute drive from home, the tester may need to warm the car up first, or the diesel smoke test may have to wait until the engine reaches 80°C. Arriving with the engine already warm saves time.

How long does a retest take?

If your car fails, the retest is usually much quicker than the full test, because it only covers the items that failed. A partial retest often takes 10 to 30 minutes, depending on how many items need rechecking.

The cost and the timing depend on where and when the retest happens. GOV.UK sets the rules: if you leave the vehicle at the test centre for repair and it is retested within 10 working days, the partial retest is free. If you take the car away and bring it back to the same test centre before the end of the next working day, a partial retest on certain items, lights, horn, mirrors, wipers, tyres and similar, is also free. Take it back within 10 working days and you can be charged a partial retest fee. After 10 working days, or at a different test centre, the car needs a full test again, at the full fee and the full 45 to 60 minutes.

Our guide to what to do if your car fails its MOT explains the retest rules, the appeal process and what you are allowed to drive while the car has no valid MOT.

Can you wait at the test centre?

Yes. GOV.UK says you can watch the test from a viewing area, but you are not allowed to interrupt the tester. Most garages have a waiting room, and for a 45 to 60 minute test most drivers simply wait on site. If you would rather not wait, some test centres collect the car and return it, ask when you book, because not every centre offers this.

One thing to avoid: do not stand next to the tester asking questions while the test is running. The tester has to concentrate, and interruptions slow the test down. If you want to understand what was checked, ask at the end, a good tester will walk you through the advisories on the certificate.

How to keep your MOT to an hour

You cannot make the test itself shorter, but you can stop it from running over. A few things before the test make a real difference:

Do the home checks first. The most common MOT failures are cheap to spot at home: lights, tyre tread and pressures, wipers, washer fluid and the horn. Run through our pre-MOT checklist, fifteen checks you can do in ten minutes, and read the most common MOT failure reasons so you know what else the tester will be looking at.

Arrive with a warm engine. Take the car for a proper run of 20 to 30 minutes before the test, so the engine is at its normal temperature when the tester starts. This matters most for diesels, where the smoke test needs at least 80°C.

Book the first slot of the day. The first appointment is the least likely to be delayed by earlier jobs running over. Saturday mornings and the last week of the month are the busiest times to book.

Fix the warning lights. Any engine, ABS or airbag warning light that stays on is a fail, and it will be recorded and discussed, which takes time. Get it diagnosed before the test, not after it.

Bring the right paperwork. You do not need the V5C logbook for the test itself, but the tester needs the registration number, and if you want a printed certificate you will need to ask for one. Having the old certificate or the V5C handy avoids delays if there is a question about the car's details.

Quick answers

How long does a standard car MOT take?

A standard class 4 car MOT takes 45 to 60 minutes of inspection time. The tester has to work through the full DVSA checklist, so a proper test cannot be done in 15 or 20 minutes.

Will I get my car back in an hour?

Not always. The test itself is 45 to 60 minutes, but the garage may keep the car longer while it waits for a free bay, for the paperwork, or for repairs if it fails. Allow a morning, or the whole day, if you are leaving the car with the garage.

How long does a motorcycle MOT take?

A motorcycle MOT is usually quicker than a car test, typically around 30 to 45 minutes, because there are fewer items to check. The maximum fee is £29.65 for a standard motorcycle.

How long does an MOT retest take?

A partial retest covers only the items that failed, so it is usually much quicker than a full test, often 10 to 30 minutes. If you leave the car at the test centre for repair and it is retested within 10 working days, the partial retest is free. After 10 working days, or at a different test centre, a full test is needed and the full fee applies.

Can I wait at the garage while the MOT is done?

Yes. You can watch the test from the viewing area, but you are not allowed to interrupt the tester. Most garages let you wait on site for the 45 to 60 minutes the test takes.

To sum up: a standard car MOT takes 45 to 60 minutes of inspection time, and that figure holds whether the car passes or fails. Motorcycles are quicker at around 30 to 45 minutes, and larger vans and minibuses take longer, up to 90 minutes. The test time is not the same as the time you will be without the car, waiting for a bay, paperwork and repairs all add on, so allow a morning if you are dropping the car off, and book the first slot of the day if you want it back quickly. A partial retest on a failed car is usually 10 to 30 minutes and is free if the car stays at the test centre and is retested within 10 working days.
